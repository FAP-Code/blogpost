## Executive Summary

> Ghana possesses the agricultural resources, the institutional frameworks, and the policy mandate to transform its rural economy. What it has consistently lacked is the governance credibility to enforce that transformation.

Ghana faces a structural economic challenge that no amount of macroeconomic stabilisation can resolve on its own: the country produces vast agricultural wealth and then systematically exports that wealth in its rawest, least valuable form. Cashew leaves as nuts; shea as kernels; rubber as latex; oil palm as crude bunches. The processing, the branding, the margin, these happen abroad. Ghana bears the environmental and labour costs of production, while importing nations capture the industrial value. This is the value leakage trap, and it has defined Ghana's agricultural economy for decades.

This paper argues that Ghana's non-traditional agricultural value chains, cashew, shea, rubber, oil palm, mango, and pineapple, remain underdeveloped not primarily because of insufficient investment, but because of governance failures, unenforced policy mandates, and fragmented institutional frameworks. Without credible regulatory reform and deliberate public-private coordination, additional capital directed at these value chains will continue to yield disappointing returns, and the diversification agenda will stall at the point of announcement rather than implementation.

### Key Findings

1. **Shifting Dependence, Not Diversifying** — Ghana is not diversifying its export base, it is replacing one raw commodity dependence with another. Gold has superseded cocoa as the leading export, but gold mining is capital-intensive, employs fewer than 4% of the workforce, and generates severe fiscal pro-cyclicality identical to cocoa.
2. **The Raw Export Trap Across All Sectors** — Ghana exports 95% of cashew, 95% of rubber, and 90% of shea in raw form. Only 6–10% of cashew is processed domestically. The country produces palm oil but imports refined palm products, spending precious foreign exchange on goods it could manufacture at home.
3. **Governance Failure, Not Investment Shortage** — Raw export bans have been announced but not enforced. Processing factories operate below capacity due to feedstock shortages caused by regulatory gaps. The One District, One Factory programme was officially discontinued after many announced facilities never became operational.
4. **Infrastructure and Finance Deficits Compound the Problem** — Post-harvest losses reach 30–40% in horticulture due to absent cold chain infrastructure. Agricultural processors cannot access long-term affordable finance through commercial banks, and the Development Bank Ghana's rural agro-processing portfolio remains underdeveloped.
5. **The $12 Billion Opportunity Requires Systems Reform** — The TCDA's 2026 blueprint projects that six priority tree crops could generate $12 billion annually by 2030. Realising this target requires not only production scaling but a coordinated institutional architecture that currently does not exist.

### Priority Recommendations

The paper prescribes five clusters of reform: mandate domestic processing floors for cashew (30% rising to 60% by 2030) paired with subsidised industrial energy; enforce the raw shea export ban and establish a price stabilisation fund protecting women processors; establish a Non-Traditional Agricultural Export Processing Fund capitalised by levies on raw exports; ring-fence feeder road budgets for the six priority TCDA production zones; and create an Agricultural Value Chain Coordination Office with authority to align MoFA, MOTI, GEPA, TCDA, and the Development Bank Ghana under a single implementation plan. Each recommendation names the responsible institution and acknowledges the key constraint to its delivery.

## 1. Introduction

Ghana produces approximately 250,000 tonnes of raw cashew nuts each year. It exports 95 percent of this output, unprocessed, to buyers in India and Vietnam, who transform the raw nuts into kernels, cashew butter, and industrial shell liquids sold globally at three to four times the farm-gate price (Tree Crops Development Authority, 2026; Ministry of Food and Agriculture, 2025). The finished products then re-enter African markets, including Ghana's own supermarket shelves, at premiums that Ghanaian processors cannot afford to charge because they were never allowed to add the value in the first place. This single case encapsulates a structural failure that repeats across cashew, shea, rubber, oil palm, mango, and pineapple: Ghana bears the land, the labour, and the environmental cost of agricultural production, while the processing margin, the engine of industrial employment and rural income growth, is captured abroad.

The structural origins of this problem lie deep in Ghana's colonial agricultural economy, which was organised around raw commodity extraction for metropolitan processing industries (Ruf, 2007). Cocoa anchored this model for most of the twentieth century, and it did so with characteristic vulnerability: boom years when global prices rose, fiscal crisis when they fell, and persistent failure to diversify regardless of which administration held power. In recent years, gold has overtaken cocoa as Ghana's primary export earner, but this shift has resolved none of the underlying structural problem. It has simply exchanged one raw commodity dependence for another, while the agricultural sector, which employs over 40 percent of the workforce, remains trapped in low-value raw production (World Bank, 2024; AfDB, 2025).

The critical research question this paper addresses is: why have Ghana's non-traditional agricultural value chains remained underdeveloped despite their demonstrated economic potential, and what targeted investments and institutional reforms can unlock them? The answer, this paper argues, lies not in the absence of investment but in the failure of governance. Ghana's policy landscape is not empty, the Tree Crops Development Authority exists, the One District, One Factory programme was launched, the Planting for Food and Jobs scheme subsidised production. The problem is that these policies were announced rather than implemented, and coordinated in name rather than in practice. As Amponsah et al. (2026) demonstrate in their econometric assessment of Ghana's agro-industrialisation frameworks, weak institutional coordination, insufficient financial support, and inadequate monitoring and evaluation have systematically undermined every major agricultural policy initiative of the past decade.

This paper makes the case that Ghana stands at a genuine strategic inflection point. The TCDA's $12 billion tree crops blueprint is the most ambitious agricultural diversification plan the country has ever produced. The government has announced bans on raw cashew, shea, and rubber exports. The IMF Extended Credit Facility provides fiscal stabilisation. The AfCFTA Secretariat sits in Accra. These conditions will not converge again. The question is whether Ghana possesses the institutional credibility to convert this moment into structural change. The analysis proceeds as follows: Section 2 establishes Ghana's export structure and the shifting-dependence problem; Section 3 makes the affirmative case for non-traditional agricultural value chains; Section 4 introduces the value chain analytical framework; Section 5 provides five case studies; Sections 6 and 7 diagnose cross-cutting constraints and institutional failures; Section 8 prescribes targeted reforms; and Sections 9 and 10 situate the argument in Ghana's broader strategic context before concluding.

## 2. Ghana's Export Structure: From Cocoa to Gold

Ghana is not diversifying its economy. It is shifting its dependence from one raw commodity to another. This distinction carries profound consequences for the country's development trajectory, its fiscal resilience, and the employment prospects of its growing population. Understanding the export structure, and its historical evolution, is the necessary foundation for any serious analysis of what genuine diversification would require.

**Table 1: Ghana Export Composition, Key Commodities**

| Commodity | Share of Exports | Employment Intensity | Processing Domestic % |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gold | ~47% | Very Low (<4% workforce) | Minimal, largely raw |
| Cocoa (raw beans) | ~20% | Moderate (smallholders) | ~30% processed locally |
| Crude Oil | ~9% | Very Low | Minimal |
| Non-Trad. Agric. | ~6% | High (rural labour) | 6–10% avg across crops |
| Other | ~18% | Varies | Varies |

*Source: Ghana Statistical Service 2024; World Bank 2024; TCDA 2026*

Gold has become the dominant force in Ghana's export economy. In 2019, Ghana overtook South Africa to become the continent's largest gold producer, and gold now accounts for approximately 47 percent of merchandise export earnings (Ghana Statistical Service, 2024). This transition is frequently presented as an economic achievement. It is more accurately described as a structural vulnerability in different clothing. Gold mining is managed by large multinational corporations with highly capital-intensive operations. The sector employs fewer than four percent of the national workforce directly, generates limited backward linkages to domestic suppliers, and creates severe fiscal pro-cyclicality, government revenues surge when prices rise and contract sharply when they fall (World Bank, 2024). Ghana experienced precisely this dynamic in 2022 when a convergence of falling commodity revenues and rising debt service costs triggered the fiscal crisis that necessitated the IMF bailout.

Cocoa, which built Ghana's modern economy, tells a similarly cautionary story. Ghana is the world's second-largest cocoa producer, generating over 800,000 tonnes annually at peak production (Ghana Statistical Service, 2024). Yet cocoa prices are structurally set by buyers in London and New York commodity markets, and the country's cocoa revenue swings by hundreds of millions of dollars between seasons, destabilising fiscal planning and rural household incomes simultaneously. The 2024 cocoa season demonstrated this fragility acutely, production shortfalls, global price volatility, and financing gaps left processors and farmers facing a liquidity crisis that threatened IMF programme compliance (SECO Economic Report, 2025).

The value leakage trap is not a metaphor, it is a quantifiable loss. When Ghana exports a kilogram of raw cashew nut at approximately $0.80–$1.00 and imports processed cashew kernel at $7–$10 per kilogram, the country is surrendering $6–9 in value per kilogram. Multiplied across 250,000 tonnes of annual cashew production, the potential annual value addition foregone reaches hundreds of millions of dollars. A similar calculation applies to shea, rubber, and oil palm. The TCDA estimates that if processing targets are met across all six priority tree crops, the sector could generate $12 billion annually, compared to current non-traditional agricultural export revenues of well under $1 billion (TCDA, 2026). The gap between these figures represents the scale of what Ghana's governance failures are costing the country each year.

## 3. Why Non-Traditional Agricultural Value Chains Matter

The case for focusing national policy attention and institutional energy on cashew, shea, rubber, oil palm, mango, and pineapple rests on five interconnected arguments: employment intensity, rural livelihood impact, gender inclusion, genuine export diversification, and industrial linkage creation. Taken together, these arguments establish that non-traditional agricultural value chains are uniquely positioned to deliver the kind of growth that gold extraction and cocoa exports structurally cannot.

**Key figures:** 40%+ of the workforce is in agriculture · $12B TCDA 2030 projection · 500K+ new jobs targeted · 6 priority tree crops

Employment intensity is the starting point. Agriculture employs over 40 percent of Ghana's workforce, with at least four in five Ghanaian farmers managing smallholder plots of less than two hectares (Farmonaut, 2025). The gold and petroleum sectors, by contrast, are capital-intensive enclaves: they generate export revenue but create relatively few jobs per dollar invested and have limited forward and backward linkages to the domestic economy. Agricultural processing is structurally different. Establishing cashew shelling plants, shea butter refineries, rubber processing facilities, and cold-chain packing houses creates employment at multiple stages, in production, aggregation, processing, quality control, packaging, logistics, and marketing. Each processing facility also generates indirect employment through input supply chains, maintenance services, and transport networks that extend deep into rural areas.

Rural livelihood impact follows directly from employment intensity. The majority of Ghana's poor population lives in rural areas and depends on agriculture for income. Processing facilities located in or near production zones, rather than in urban industrial parks disconnected from the farm, transform smallholder farmers from price-taking commodity sellers into supply chain participants with more stable and higher incomes. This is the mechanism through which agricultural value chain development reduces rural poverty: it raises the price received per unit of agricultural output by capturing the value addition that currently accrues to processors in India, Vietnam, and Europe.

Gender inclusion is a structurally embedded advantage of several of these value chains, particularly shea. Shea processing in Ghana's northern regions is almost entirely conducted by women, with women collectors, processors, and cooperative managers forming the backbone of the industry (Awo and Anaman, 2015; Opoku-Mensah et al., 2024). Developing the shea value chain is therefore not merely an agricultural policy, it is a gender equity intervention. Research by Wageningen UR confirms that women in cashew and shea processing groups earn substantially higher incomes than non-participants, investing in housing, business expansion, and community lending (van den Berg, 2015). Policy that strengthens these value chains delivers outsized social returns relative to its cost.

Export diversification, genuine diversification, not the commodity-shifting described in Section 2, requires building value-added exports across multiple sectors with different price cycles. Processed cashew, shea butter, refined rubber, certified palm oil, and packaged tropical fruits do not move in concert with gold prices or cocoa harvests. A portfolio of processed agricultural exports therefore provides macroeconomic buffers that raw commodity dependence structurally cannot. Finally, agricultural processing creates industrial linkages, demand for packaging materials, laboratory testing, cold storage technology, agricultural machinery, and transport infrastructure, that build the broader industrial base Ghana needs for long-term structural transformation (Owoo and Lambon-Quayefio, 2017). Gold extraction creates no comparable domestic industrial demand.

## 4. Analytical Framework: The Value Chain Approach

A value chain encompasses all activities required to bring a product from its inception through production, transformation, and delivery to the final consumer, and the set of firms, workers, and institutions involved in those activities (Kaplinsky and Morris, 2001). For agricultural commodities, the relevant stages are: inputs and primary production; post-harvest handling and aggregation; processing and value addition; logistics and transport; standards and certification compliance; and final market access. Understanding which actors capture value at each stage, and why, is the analytical core of this paper.

**Table 2: Value Chain Stages and Ghana's Current Participation**

| Stage | Ghana's Role | Value Captured | Constraint |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inputs & Production | Active, smallholders | Low, farm-gate price only | Low productivity, climate |
| Post-Harvest Handling | Weak, minimal investment | Minimal | Post-harvest losses 30–40% |
| Processing & Value Add | Very Limited (6–10%) | Very Low | Feedstock shortage, energy cost |
| Logistics & Transport | Partial | Low | Poor roads, no cold chain |
| Standards & Certification | Underdeveloped | Low | EU SPS, EUDR compliance gaps |
| Market Access | Raw commodity markets only | Low | Excluded from premium markets |

*Source: Gereffi 2018; UNIDO 2024; TCDA 2026; author's synthesis*

The critical concept for this analysis is value capture, the share of total chain revenue that accrues to domestic actors. Ghana currently captures value almost exclusively at the first stage: raw production at the farm gate. The high-margin stages, processing, branding, and retail, are performed in importing countries. This means that Ghanaian farmers and the Ghanaian state absorb the full cost of land use, labour, environmental degradation, and climate risk, while foreign processors collect the industrial premium. Value chain upgrading, the analytical objective of this paper, refers to the process of acquiring the institutional, technological, and regulatory capacity to move domestic actors into higher-margin activities (Gereffi, 2018).

A further concept essential to understanding Ghana's position is buyer-driven governance. Global agricultural value chains are not governed by neutral market forces, they are governed by powerful lead firms, typically multinational processors, traders, and retailers, who set prices, standards, and terms of trade for producers in developing countries (Gereffi, 2018; Opoku-Mensah et al., 2024). These lead firms have no structural incentive to relocate processing to Ghana, they benefit from Ghana's position as a raw material supplier. This means that market forces alone cannot drive value chain upgrading. Decisive state intervention, through processing mandates, export restrictions, infrastructure investment, and institutional coordination, is a necessary precondition for Ghana to move up the value chain. This is not a market failure requiring minor correction; it is a structural feature of how global commodity chains are organised, and it requires a correspondingly structural policy response.

## 5. Case Studies: High-Potential Value Chains

Each case study below reveals the same structural pattern: significant agronomic potential trapped at the raw export stage by a combination of perverse incentives, governance gaps, and infrastructure deficits. The analytical task is to identify the distinctive constraint in each sector that policy must address first.

### 5.1 Cashew

Cashew has become Ghana's most significant non-traditional agricultural export, produced primarily in the transitional ecological zones of Brong-Ahafo, Volta, and Northern regions. Annual production stands at approximately 250,000 tonnes of raw nuts, with the TCDA targeting a scale-up to 600,000 tonnes by 2030 (TCDA, 2026). At full processing volumes, a mature cashew industry generates not only edible kernels but cashew shell liquid, used in industrial coatings, brake linings, and resin production, creating a multi-product industrial cluster from a single agricultural input. The economic case for domestic processing is overwhelming on paper.

**Table 3: Ghana Cashew Sector, Key Indicators**

| Indicator | Current | TCDA 2030 Target |
|---|---|---|
| Annual Production | 250,000 tonnes | 600,000 tonnes |
| Processed Domestically | 6–10% | 60% |
| Direct Processing Jobs | ~3,000 | 100,000+ |
| Annual Export Earnings | ~$100M (raw) | $660M+ (processed) |

*Source: TCDA 2026; Ministry of Food and Agriculture 2025; African Cashew Alliance 2024*

The reality is a study in governance failure. Only 6–10 percent of Ghana's raw cashew is processed domestically, with the overwhelming majority exported directly to India and Vietnam where traders pay immediate spot prices that local processors cannot match (IMANI Africa, 2025). The structural mechanism is the feedstock dilemma: foreign buyers, backed by international trading capital, purchase raw nuts at the farm gate before domestic processors can secure supply. Local factories are then left without enough raw material to operate at viable capacity. The case of Usibras, Ghana's largest cashew processor, is the defining symbol of this failure. After nearly a decade of operation, Usibras considered relocation to Côte d'Ivoire, citing chronic raw material shortages, high industrial energy costs, and export tariff structures that inadvertently reward raw exports over domestic processing (IMANI Africa, 2025). A country's largest processor contemplating exit is not a market signal, it is a governance alarm.

### 5.2 Shea

Ghana ranked as the world's top exporter of shea butter in 2023, earning $90–100 million in foreign exchange, a position built on the labour of hundreds of thousands of women collectors and processors in the Northern, Upper East, and Upper West regions (News Ghana, 2025). Shea nuts are harvested from wild parkland trees that cannot be cultivated in the conventional sense; the trees grow naturally in the savannah and are managed communally. This means the sector's supply base cannot be rapidly expanded through planting programmes, making supply governance and raw nut allocation between exporters and local processors a critical policy lever.

The 2024 season exposed the fragility of Ghana's shea position with striking clarity. Production fell 45 percent below projections due to climate stress and collection pressure, yet the government permitted exporters to ship nearly half of the available nuts, leaving domestic processors without the feedstock needed to fulfil their contracts (OTI-GATI, 2025). Between 2023 and mid-2024, the price of a 100-kilogram bag of shea nuts increased by 140 percent, making it economically impossible for artisanal processors to compete with foreign buyers (OTI-GATI, 2025). Companies including Organic Trade and Investments were forced to reduce shea butter production by over 60 percent of standard monthly capacity. Ghana's revenue from shea butter is estimated to have declined by over 40 percent during this period, a loss measured not only in export earnings but in the incomes of the women who depend on shea processing for their livelihoods. The government's announced ban on raw shea nut exports has not been consistently enforced, exposing a gap between policy declaration and regulatory reality that sits at the heart of this paper's central argument.

### 5.3 Rubber

Ghana's rubber sector produces approximately 145,000 tonnes of natural rubber annually, concentrated in the Western, Central, and Eastern regions. Like cashew, it exports approximately 95 percent of its output in raw latex form, foregoing the processing margin on natural rubber sheets, technically specified rubber, and derivative products used in tyre manufacturing, footwear, medical equipment, and industrial goods (Ministry of Food and Agriculture, 2025). The TCDA targets scaling production to 400,000 tonnes, nearly a tripling of current output, but production scaling without processing reform simply amplifies the existing value leakage rather than correcting it.

The rubber sector shares cashew's feedstock dilemma but has an additional constraint: industrial energy costs. Rubber processing requires reliable, affordable electricity for compound mixing, vulcanisation, and quality testing. Ghana's history of power supply instability, dumsor, has made energy a direct cost competitiveness factor that local processors cannot overcome without targeted policy support. Emmanuel Akwasi Owusu of the Association of Rubber Actors of Ghana proposed at the 2025 Ghana Industrial Summit a trial suspension of raw rubber exports for three to six months to test whether local processors can absorb supply if raw exports are restricted (IMANI Africa, 2025). This proposal, essentially a controlled policy experiment, has not been acted upon. The pattern repeating across cashew, shea, and rubber is no coincidence: it reflects a systemic preference among policymakers for announcing export bans rather than enforcing them.

### 5.4 Horticulture: Mango and Pineapple

Ghana's horticulture sector is its fastest-growing agricultural export sub-sector, led by pineapple cultivation in the Eastern and Central regions and mango production in the northern transitional zones (Farmonaut, 2025; WACOMP, 2019). The TCDA targets scaling mango output from 150,000 to 450,000 tonnes and coconut from 504,000 to one million tonnes by 2030. Pineapple, particularly the MD2 variety now dominant in global markets, has established export relationships with European buyers and demonstrated that Ghanaian smallholders can meet international quality requirements when supported with appropriate inputs and market linkages (Annor et al., 2025).

The binding constraint in horticulture is infrastructure, not production capacity. Post-harvest losses are estimated at 30–40 percent for some horticultural crops, occurring primarily between the farm and the processing facility or port due to the absence of a functional cold chain (HortiFresh, 2021). A mango or pineapple that rots in transit is not merely a lost sale, it is a lost investment in land preparation, inputs, labour, and water. EU phytosanitary standards, including the EU Deforestation Regulation that entered force in 2024 and imposes supply chain traceability requirements, present both a compliance burden and a market opportunity. Ghanaian exporters who cannot afford GlobalGAP certification, or who lack the testing infrastructure to demonstrate pesticide residue compliance, are locked out of the premium European market segments that command the highest prices (Annor et al., 2025). Without public investment in cold chain infrastructure, rural road rehabilitation, and certification support for smallholders, production scaling in horticulture will continue to generate spoilage rather than export earnings.

### 5.5 Oil Palm

Oil palm presents the starkest manifestation of the import paradox in Ghana's agricultural economy. Ghana produces crude palm oil from wild groves, smallholder plots, and estate plantations concentrated in the Western, Central, and Eastern forest belt. But domestic production falls well short of consumption needs, Ghana imports refined palm oil products at significant foreign exchange cost, spending scarce reserves on a commodity that grows indigenously and for which the country has significant agronomic and land resource advantages (FAO, 2013; IMANI Africa, 2025). The TCDA's most ambitious target is scaling oil palm production from 265,000 to 1.1 million tonnes, reflecting the sector's potential and the depth of current underperformance.

The One District, One Factory initiative was explicitly designed to address oil palm processing gaps, among other sectors. Its performance in this regard has been limited. Research by Amponsah et al. (2026) using macro-econometric time series analysis confirms that weak institutional coordination, insufficient financial support, and inadequate monitoring and evaluation undermined 1D1F's impact across sectors. The programme's official discontinuation has left a policy vacuum. Smallholder productivity in oil palm is approximately one-quarter of estate-level yields, reflecting limited access to improved planting material, fertiliser, and extension services (FAO, 2013). Sustainability certification under the Roundtable on Sustainable Palm Oil is increasingly required by European buyers but remains inaccessible to most Ghanaian smallholders due to cost and complexity. The import paradox will persist as long as processing investment is not paired with smallholder productivity support and supply chain governance reform.

## 6. Cross-Cutting Constraints

The five case studies above reveal a structural pattern that transcends individual commodities. The same constraints appear, with only minor variation, across cashew, shea, rubber, horticulture, and oil palm. This section synthesises those constraints at the systems level, arguing that they are mutually reinforcing and that no single-sector or single-instrument intervention can break the cycle. A systems-level policy response is the necessary conclusion.

### 6.1 Agro-Processing Gaps and the Raw Material Incentive Problem

The most visible constraint across all value chains is the gap between raw material production and domestic processing capacity. But describing this gap as an investment shortage misidentifies its cause. Farmers and aggregators sell raw commodities to foreign buyers not because they lack patriotism or developmental consciousness, but because foreign buyers offer immediate cash liquidity at prices that domestic processors, constrained by energy costs, financing costs, and operational inefficiencies, cannot match. This is a rational response to a perverse incentive structure created by the absence of export regulations, processing mandates, and feedstock allocation mechanisms. The market, operating freely, will continue to produce this outcome regardless of how many processing factories are built. Only deliberate policy intervention, through minimum domestic processing requirements, export permit systems tied to local supply obligations, or price stabilisation mechanisms, can alter the incentive structure sufficiently to make domestic processing consistently viable.

### 6.2 Infrastructure and Logistics Deficits

Agricultural value chains cannot function without physical infrastructure, and Ghana's rural infrastructure deficit is a direct tax on every stage of the value chain. Poor feeder roads increase transport costs from farm to aggregation point, damage produce in transit, and make it economically unviable to source raw materials from remote production areas. The absence of cold chain infrastructure, refrigerated transport, pack houses, blast chillers, and controlled-atmosphere storage, means that horticultural exports lose 30–40 percent of production value to spoilage before reaching the port. Electricity unreliability raises processing costs for rubber, cashew, and shea producers, making their finished goods structurally uncompetitive against processed imports from countries with stable industrial power supply. These infrastructure deficits are not peripheral concerns, they are foundational constraints that make value chain development economically impossible regardless of processing investment.

### 6.3 Access to Long-Term Finance

Processing investment requires patient capital: long-term loans at interest rates that allow factories to build operational capacity before generating returns. Ghana's commercial banking sector has historically offered precisely the opposite, short-term credit at high interest rates, collateral requirements that smallholder farmers and small processors cannot meet, and a risk aversion toward agricultural lending that reflects genuine sector volatility. The Development Bank Ghana was established in 2021 with an explicit mandate to fill this gap. Its rural agricultural processing portfolio remains, however, underdeveloped relative to its mandate, with the bank's risk appetite gravitating toward less complex urban commercial lending. Blended finance, combining the DBG's balance sheet with concessional donor capital, and supplementing commercial lending with targeted grant instruments for smallholder cooperative development, represents the most feasible path to closing the agricultural finance gap at the scale required.

### 6.4 Standards, Certification, and the EUDR Challenge

Access to premium export markets requires compliance with increasingly demanding standards frameworks. The EU's sanitary and phytosanitary regulations, GlobalGAP certification requirements for fresh produce, and the Roundtable on Sustainable Palm Oil standards for oil palm impose documentation, testing, and traceability obligations that most Ghanaian smallholders and small processors cannot meet independently. The EU Deforestation Regulation, which entered force in 2024, adds a new layer of supply chain traceability requirements for cocoa, coffee, and palm oil, with cascading implications for other agricultural commodities. Ghana's Export Promotion Authority provides market intelligence and promotion but lacks the technical capacity and resources to systematically support value chain actors through standards compliance processes. Digital traceability systems, increasingly deployed in Côte d'Ivoire and Kenya, represent an emerging competitive advantage that Ghana has not yet captured at scale.

### 6.5 Institutional Fragmentation and Coordination Failure

The deepest constraint is institutional. Responsibility for agricultural value chain development in Ghana is distributed across the Ministry of Food and Agriculture, the Ministry of Trade, Agribusiness and Industry, the Ghana Export Promotion Authority, the Tree Crops Development Authority, the Development Bank Ghana, commodity boards, and district assemblies. Each body has its own mandate, budget cycle, and performance metrics. Coordination between them is ad hoc, driven by personal relationships between officials rather than systemic institutional architecture. The result is that policies announced by one ministry are undermined by the actions of another: GEPA promotes raw commodity exports at the same time TCDA advocates for processing mandates; infrastructure ministries prioritise urban road construction while agricultural feeder roads deteriorate; the DBG pursues commercial viability targets that direct lending away from the rural agro-processors who need it most. This fragmentation means that even well-designed individual policies fail at implementation, because no single institution has the authority or the incentive alignment to coordinate the system as a whole.

> These five constraints are mutually reinforcing. The financing gap prevents processors from absorbing raw material costs elevated by the feedstock dilemma; infrastructure deficits raise operating costs that already-stressed processors cannot afford; standards compliance is impossible without cold chain investment that finance constraints prevent; and institutional fragmentation ensures that no coordinated response addresses any of these interconnections. Breaking this cycle requires a systems-level policy intervention, not a single instrument, but an integrated package of reforms designed around their interdependencies.

## 7. Policy Gaps and Institutional Challenges

Ghana does not lack agricultural policy. It lacks policy credibility, the demonstrated institutional capacity to implement announced commitments, enforce regulatory mandates, and sustain reform momentum beyond the electoral cycle. This section assesses the country's major agricultural institutions against this standard, distinguishing between design failures, resource failures, and political economy failures that have limited their impact.

**Table 4: Institutional Assessment, Ghana's Key Agricultural Bodies**

| Institution | Design | Resourcing | Implementation | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| TCDA | Strong | Moderate | Weak, enforcement gaps | Credibility gap |
| MoFA / PFJ | Good at production | Moderate | Weak market linkages | Partial success |
| GEPA | Promotional only | Weak | Limited mandate | Structurally inadequate |
| 1D1F Programme | Ambitious | Underfunded | Poor, discontinued | Policy failure |
| Development Bank Ghana | Well-designed | Moderate | Urban bias | Under-deployed |

*Source: Amponsah et al. 2026; World Bank 2024; IMANI Africa 2025; author's assessment*

The Tree Crops Development Authority represents the most structurally ambitious institutional response to the value chain problem. Established under Act 1010 of 2019, it has a mandate to regulate and develop cashew, shea, mango, coconut, rubber, and oil palm across their full value chains. Its 2026 investment blueprint is the most comprehensive agricultural diversification plan Ghana has produced. The critical question is whether the TCDA has the enforcement capacity to implement it. The pattern so far suggests not: raw export bans have been announced but not consistently enforced; processing registration requirements exist but compliance monitoring is weak; the feedstock allocation mechanisms needed to protect domestic processors from foreign buyer competition have not been operationalised. The TCDA's challenge is not one of vision, it is one of regulatory muscle.

The Ministry of Food and Agriculture's Planting for Food and Jobs programme achieved measurable success in its defined domain: input subsidisation at the farm production stage. It distributed improved seeds and fertiliser to smallholder farmers at scale, and contributed to measured output increases in several crop categories (Ibrahim, 2025). Its structural weakness was equally clear: the programme invested in the supply end of the value chain with no parallel investment in processing capacity, storage infrastructure, or market linkages. The consequence was predictable, harvest gluts without processing capacity caused farm-gate price collapses that eliminated the income gains PFJ had generated at the production stage. A programme designed to reach farmers stopped at the farm gate.

The One District, One Factory initiative illustrates the danger of political industrialisation, the tendency to announce factory construction as a political deliverable without ensuring the supply chain, financing, and market conditions needed to make factories operational and viable. Amponsah et al. (2026) found that many 1D1F facilities were non-operational, disconnected from local agricultural supply chains, or stranded by the feedstock shortages that the programme itself had no mechanism to address. The programme's official discontinuation by the current administration confirms what the evidence showed: announcing processing investment without resolving the raw material incentive problem produces stranded assets, not industrial transformation.

The Development Bank Ghana was created with an explicit mandate to provide long-term developmental finance to sectors, including agriculture, that commercial banks systematically underserve. Its deployment to date has been conservative relative to its mandate, with portfolio allocation gravitating toward lower-risk urban commercial lending rather than the higher-risk, higher-impact rural agro-processing investments it was designed to support (World Bank, 2024). Changing this requires not simply encouragement but structural reform: clear portfolio allocation targets for agricultural processing, concessional capital from development finance institutions to offset risk, and technical assistance to help small agro-processors develop bankable business plans.

## 8. Policy Recommendations

Each recommendation below identifies the responsible institution, the specific action required, the target timeframe, and the key implementation constraint. Vague prescriptions are not included. The standard applied is: would a senior official reading this know exactly what to do next Monday morning?

### 8.1 Value-Chain-Specific Strategies

**R1 — Cashew: Mandate Domestic Processing Floors**
The TCDA must immediately mandate that a minimum of 30 percent of annual raw cashew production be processed domestically before export, rising to 60 percent by 2030. This mandate must be operationalised through an export permit system administered by the TCDA: exporters of raw cashew must demonstrate compliance with the domestic processing floor before permits are issued. The mandate must be paired with subsidised industrial energy tariffs for licensed processors through the Energy Commission, and a TCDA-administered feedstock allocation register that matches available raw nut supply to licensed domestic processors before permitting raw exports. The key implementation constraint is political pressure from raw nut exporters and their foreign buyers, this must be anticipated and managed through clear public communication of the enforcement timeline. *Responsible institution: TCDA, Ministry of Energy, Ghana Revenue Authority. Target: Q4 2026.*

**R2 — Shea: Enforce the Export Ban and Protect Women Processors**
The announced ban on raw shea nut exports during the processing season must be enforced with the same regulatory seriousness as the cocoa farmgate price mechanism. The TCDA must establish a Shea Nut Price Stabilisation Fund, modelled on the Cocoa Stabilisation Mechanism, capitalised by a levy on existing raw shea nut exports, to shield women processors from the extreme price volatility that destroyed processing capacity in 2024. Community-level shea processing centres must be established in Ghana's three northern regions, co-financed by the Development Bank Ghana and managed by women's cooperatives. Concurrently, the Carbon Markets Office under the EPA should begin registering shea tree parklands as carbon assets, with pilot credit authorisations targeting rural cooperative beneficiaries. *Responsible institution: TCDA, DBG, EPA Carbon Markets Office. Target: enforcement by Q2 2026; fund operational Q4 2026.*

**R3 — Rubber: Commission the Export Trial and Design a Permanent Framework**
The Association of Rubber Actors' proposal for a 3–6 month trial suspension of raw rubber exports must be commissioned as a formal policy experiment, with monitoring conducted by the TCDA and the Ghana Statistical Service to measure domestic processor absorption capacity. Results must inform a permanent feedstock allocation framework. Energy tariff concessions for rubber processors must be introduced through the Energy Commission, with eligibility tied to TCDA processing certification. *Responsible institution: TCDA, Ghana Statistical Service, Energy Commission. Target: trial commencing Q3 2026.*

**R4 — Horticulture: Cold Chain First, Then Market Access**
The Ministry of Roads and Highways must ring-fence a minimum of 8 percent of the annual road fund for feeder road rehabilitation in the Eastern Region pineapple corridor and the Northern Region mango zones, the two highest-value horticultural export corridors. Public-private partnerships for cold chain infrastructure must be structured such that the government provides land, utility connections, and road access, while private operators manage refrigerated storage and pack-house facilities. The Ghana Export Promotion Authority must establish a Phytosanitary Compliance Support Fund to co-finance smallholder GlobalGAP certification and EU Deforestation Regulation traceability system registration. *Responsible institution: Ministry of Roads, GEPA, Ministry of Agriculture. Target: PPP structures tendered Q1 2027.*

**R5 — Oil Palm: Link Certification to Smallholder Clusters**
The TCDA must accelerate RSPO certification of oil palm smallholder clusters in the Western, Central, and Eastern regions as a precondition for linking them to 1D1F-successor processing facilities. The Ministry of Trade should negotiate preferential import tariffs on refined palm oil from RSPO-certified Ghanaian exporters with key trading partners under the AfCFTA framework, creating a market pull for certified domestic production. Smallholder productivity extension must be resourced through MoFA's district offices, with a specific mandate to reduce the four-fold productivity gap between smallholders and estate operations. *Responsible institution: TCDA, Ministry of Trade, MoFA. Target: cluster certification programme launched Q2 2026.*

### 8.2 Cross-Cutting Institutional and Finance Reforms

**R6 — Establish an Agricultural Value Chain Coordination Office**
Parliament should establish an Agricultural Value Chain Coordination Office (AVCCO) within the Office of the President, with the legal authority to issue binding coordination directives to MoFA, MOTI, GEPA, TCDA, and DBG, and to hold each institution accountable against quarterly value chain performance targets. This is the single most important institutional reform recommended in this paper. Without coordination authority at the centre of government, the fragmentation diagnosed in Section 7 will persist regardless of how well individual institutions perform. *Responsible institution: Office of the President, Parliament. Target: legislation tabled Q1 2027.*

**R7 — Direct the Development Bank Ghana to Prioritise Agro-Processing**
The DBG's Board must formally adopt a minimum portfolio allocation of 30 percent of annual lending to rural agro-processing, with lending terms of 7–15 years at concessional rates for smallholder cooperatives and women-led enterprises. The Ministry of Finance should negotiate a blended finance facility, combining DBG capital with concessional funding from IFC, AfDB, and bilateral development finance institutions, specifically targeting the $1–10 million investment range that commercial banks and large DFIs both currently ignore. *Responsible institution: DBG Board, Ministry of Finance. Target: portfolio mandate adopted Q3 2026; blended facility structured Q1 2027.*

**R8 — Create a Non-Traditional Agricultural Export Processing Fund**
The Ministry of Finance should establish a Non-Traditional Agricultural Export Processing Fund, capitalised by a 3 percent levy on raw commodity exports across all TCDA priority sectors. The fund should be ring-fenced, independently governed, and deployed exclusively for processing infrastructure investment, energy cost subsidies for licensed processors, and smallholder certification support. This mechanism creates a fiscal feedback loop in which the continuation of raw exports directly finances the infrastructure and incentives needed to eventually eliminate them. *Responsible institution: Ministry of Finance, TCDA. Target: fund design Q3 2026; first capitalisation Q4 2026.*

## 9. Ghana at a Strategic Crossroads

Ghana has arrived at this juncture before. The 1983 Economic Recovery Programme, the HIPC debt relief of 2001, the oil discovery of 2007, the return to the IMF in 2015, each represented a moment when external conditions created space for structural transformation, and each was followed by a period in which the opportunity was partially realised before political economy pressures reasserted the status quo. The question that defines this moment is whether the 2026 policy configuration, the TCDA blueprint, the raw export ban announcements, the IMF Extended Credit Facility, the AfCFTA Secretariat in Accra, and the Ghana-China end-harvest agreement, is substantively different from its predecessors, or whether it will follow the same cycle of ambitious announcement and limited implementation.

There are genuine structural features of the current moment that distinguish it from previous inflection points. The TCDA's $12 billion blueprint is the first agricultural diversification plan that is simultaneously specific in its commodity targets, quantified in its projections, and institutionally anchored in a statutory regulatory body. The announcement of raw export bans across cashew, shea, and rubber at a single public forum, the Ghana Tree Crops Investment Summit of February 2026, signals at minimum a political commitment that is harder to quietly reverse than regulatory changes made at the administrative level. The AfCFTA, with Ghana as its institutional home, creates a market access architecture for processed agricultural exports to continental buyers that did not exist in previous diversification cycles. And Ghana's macroeconomic position, with inflation declining sharply from a 2022 peak of over 54 percent to below 6 percent by end-2024, debt restructuring substantially complete, and real GDP growth returning to above 5 percent, provides the fiscal stability that earlier reform episodes lacked.

The inclusive growth dimension of this moment deserves particular emphasis. Ghana's population is growing rapidly, with a median age below 22 years. The gold sector, the petroleum sector, and financial services combined cannot absorb the labour force entrants that Ghana will produce over the next decade. Non-traditional agricultural value chain development is not merely an export strategy, it is the most viable employment strategy available to a country where 40 percent of the current workforce is already in agriculture and where the rural poor need income growth, not urbanisation. When a shea processing cooperative in Tamale employs 200 women and raises their average income by 60 percent, or when a cashew processing facility in Brong-Ahafo creates 500 jobs in a district that has no alternative formal employment, these are not marginal agricultural policy outcomes, they are the core of what inclusive growth means in practice.

The AfCFTA dimension adds a strategic layer that previous diversification agendas lacked. Ghana hosts the AfCFTA Secretariat and is uniquely positioned to leverage this institutional proximity into trade facilitation advantages for its processed agricultural exports. Processed cashew kernels, shea butter, refined palm oil, and packaged tropical fruit have significant market potential across the continent's 1.3 billion consumers, markets that are growing, geographically proximate, and increasingly accessible under the AfCFTA tariff reduction schedule. Building Ghana's value-added agricultural export base for African markets, not only European ones, is a strategic diversification within diversification that could insulate the country from the EU regulatory pressures, including the EUDR, that increasingly constrain raw commodity access to European markets.

## 10. Conclusion

Ghana's non-traditional agricultural value chains, cashew, shea, rubber, oil palm, mango, and pineapple, are not underdeveloped because the country lacks agronomic potential, market access, or investor interest. They are underdeveloped because governance has consistently failed to enforce the processing mandates that would make domestic value addition economically viable; because infrastructure investment has not kept pace with production growth; because institutional fragmentation has prevented coordinated policy from being sustained across electoral cycles; and because the political economy of raw commodity export has repeatedly overwhelmed the developmental logic of processing investment.

The pattern across all five case studies is structurally identical. Significant productive capacity exists. Foreign buyers offer prices that make raw exports immediately attractive. Domestic processors cannot compete for feedstock. Factories operate below capacity or close. The government announces a policy intervention, a processing mandate, an export ban, an investment programme. The intervention is announced publicly. It is not enforced consistently. The pattern repeats. This is not bad luck. It is a governance equilibrium that reflects the interests of actors, foreign trading companies, domestic commodity aggregators, politically connected export licence holders, who benefit from the current structure and who have the resources and proximity to power to resist change.

Breaking this equilibrium requires more than better policies. It requires institutional reform that changes the incentive structure for the actors who implement policy: a coordination office with real authority, processing mandates with enforceable permit systems, a price stabilisation fund that makes domestic processing commercially viable when global buyers push up raw material prices, and a financing mechanism that provides the patient capital that commercial banks will not offer. These are not technically complex interventions. They are politically consequential ones, because they redistribute the agricultural processing margin from foreign buyers and domestic aggregators to Ghanaian farmers, workers, and processors.

The cost of continued underdevelopment is not abstract. It is measured in the income that women shea processors in Tamale did not earn in 2024 because their factories ran out of shea nuts. It is measured in the jobs that Usibras's near-relocation threatened in Brong-Ahafo. It is measured in the youth who migrate from northern Ghana's cashew belt to Accra, not because cashew farming cannot support a livelihood, but because Ghana's policy failures ensure that the processing jobs that would make it viable are located in Vietnam instead. Ghana must make a different choice, and it must make it now, while the policy window is open, the macroeconomic foundations are stable, and the institutional architecture for change is in place. The $12 billion projection is not a guarantee. It is a horizon. Whether Ghana reaches it depends entirely on whether this government has the credibility, the coordination, and the political will to enforce what it has already announced.
