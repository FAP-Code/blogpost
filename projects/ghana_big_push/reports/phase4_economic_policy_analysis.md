# Phase 4: Economic and Policy Analysis

**Status: draft, feeding into `reports/full_report.md` in Phase 5.** This document does the
qualitative/analytical work the brief calls for — theory of change, comparative analysis,
implementation risk assessment, illustrative cost-benefit framing — grounded strictly in what
Phases 1–3 actually established, not filled in with plausible-sounding assumptions. Where the
evidence runs out, that's stated, not papered over.

## 1. Theory of change

A public-infrastructure programme like Big Push is supposed to work through a specific causal
chain. Making that chain explicit is what lets us later ask "which link is actually supported by
evidence, and which is just assumed?" — rather than treating "we spent money on roads" and

**Where this chain is well-evidenced today:** the Inputs and Activities columns — budget figures,
procurement method splits, and project counts are documented (with the caveats in
`sources.md`'s discrepancy log).

**Where it is weakly evidenced:** the Outputs column is partial — only 17 named projects have any
public detail, and only a handful have disclosed completion percentages. Actual km built/
rehabilitated to date, programme-wide, is not something this research can currently state with
confidence.

**Where it is essentially unevidenced yet:** everything from Outcomes onward. No before/after
travel-time, market-access, or local-employment data tied to a specific completed Big Push segment
was found in Phases 1–3. This is not a gap in our search effort so much as a structural fact about
the programme's age — most projects are, per their own reported completion percentages (6% for
Dambai as of June 2026; "13 projects at least 50% complete" programme-wide as of the same date),
too early in construction for outcome data to exist yet. **Any claim about Big Push's employment,
market-access, or poverty effects at this stage is necessarily a projection based on the theory of
change above, not a measured result** — and the final report will say so explicitly rather than
present projected effects as findings.

## 2. Comparative analysis

### 2.1 Sinohydro Bauxite Barter Arrangement (2018– ), the closest domestic comparator

| Dimension | Sinohydro | Big Push |
|---|---|---|
| Total commitment | $2bn | ~$10bn headline (contested — see discrepancy log) |
| Financing mechanism | Barter (refined bauxite for infrastructure, no cash) | Mixed: domestic budget allocation (government's stated primary claim) + at least one confirmed external credit (GMACP, $500m World Bank IDA, relationship to Big Push unresolved) |
| Regional scope | All 16 regions (by design) | All 16 regions (by design) |
| First-tranche pace | Construction began <18 months after deal signing | Contracts awarded steadily through 2025–2026; some projects (Accra–Kumasi Expressway) still in feasibility/procurement as of mid-2026 |
| Political contestation | Criticized by the same opposition figure (Kwame Agbodza) now scrutinizing Big Push, who called it "a fiasco" warranting termination; environmental criticism over bauxite mining | Criticized by Parliamentary Minority (contract disclosure, unit-cost-per-km), GhIE (independent audit petition), GHA's own workers (equipment/logistics capacity) |
| Independent audit status | Not established in this research pass (Phase 6+ task if pursued) | No completed audit as of this writing; GhIE petition pending |

**What this comparison actually tells us:** Ghana has run a nationwide, all-16-regions
infrastructure programme once already in the last decade, financed through an unconventional
mechanism, and it drew structurally similar political criticism (the same MP, on similar grounds —
financing opacity and follow-through) to what Big Push is drawing now. That is a genuine,
citable pattern — not "Ghana infrastructure programmes fail," which the evidence gathered here does
not establish either way, but "the same political fault lines reopen around each one," which it
does support.

### 2.2 Peer African programmes — an honest limitation, not a forced comparison

No programme sharing Big Push's specific structure (a named, single-administration, multi-sector,
nationwide roads-led push, financed primarily through reprioritized domestic budget) was found
in other African countries during Phases 1–3. The candidates surfaced (LAPSSET, Nigeria's Lekki
Port/Lagos–Ibadan rail, Ethiopia's growth strategy) are large infrastructure efforts but differ in
structure: LAPSSET is multilateral and corridor-specific, not a single country's domestic budget
push; Nigeria's and Ethiopia's examples are individual flagship projects or broader growth
strategies, not a single named "big push" umbrella programme comparable in scope.

**Rather than force a comparison that doesn't hold, the final report's comparative section will:**
(a) use the Sinohydro comparison as the primary within-country baseline, since it's genuinely
structurally similar; (b) draw on the development-economics "big push" literature (Rosenstein-Rodan's
original coordination-failure argument, and its later African applications) for the theoretical
frame, rather than a specific peer country's programme; (c) state directly that no like-for-like
peer programme was identified, rather than manufacture a comparison table that implies one exists.

## 3. Implementation risk assessment

Organized by risk category, each grounded in a specific Phase 1–3 finding, not a generic risk
checklist.

### 3.1 Debt sustainability
- Ghana's debt-to-GDP ratio was 42.2% as of February 2026 (Bank of Ghana), against a legislated
  45%-of-GDP anchor by 2034 under the IMF's 36-month Policy Coordination Instrument.
- Capital spending was only 1.4% of GDP as of December 2025 — a data point worth holding next to
  the GH₵30.8bn 2026 Big Push allocation: if that allocation is executed in full, it would need to
  represent a substantial jump in capital spending's GDP share, which is itself a testable
  question for Phase 5 once 2026 fiscal outturn data exists.
- Government's own framing ("no external debt contracted" for GHA-executed contracts) is in
  tension with the confirmed $500m World Bank IDA credit (GMACP) discussed in the same period —
  whether this represents actual fiscal risk depends on whether GMACP debt service is counted
  against the same debt anchor, a question this research could not resolve without the primary
  IMF DSA document (found but not fetchable in this environment).
- **Risk framing, not a risk score:** the fiscal envelope is real and currently disciplined
  (moderate debt distress risk per the IMF's own June 2026 assessment), but Big Push represents a
  large, multi-year draw against exactly the fiscal space the IMF programme was built to protect.
  Whether that draw stays within the programme's parameters is a 2026–2028 question, not
  something resolvable from data available today.

### 3.2 Procurement and value for money
- Two directly conflicting characterizations exist in the same period: a government/PPA-linked
  investigation concluding no procurement abuse, versus GhIE's formal Auditor-General petition and
  Minority demands for full contract disclosure, both citing the volume of single-source and
  restricted-tender awards (66 single-source + 51 restricted tender, of 140 projects, by one
  count).
- The National Roads Authority Act (2024) — which the Minority says would strengthen independent
  procurement oversight — is reported as not yet operationalized, more than a year after passage.
- **This is the single clearest "contradicted" item in the discrepancy log**, and the final
  report will present it as exactly that: two named, attributable positions in active dispute,
  not a resolved fact in either direction.

### 3.3 Delivery capacity
- Ghana Highway Authority's own workers publicly warned (May 2026) that obsolete equipment and
  poor logistics could undermine the programme — an internal-capacity risk distinct from
  financing or procurement, and notable because it comes from the implementing agency's own
  staff, not an external critic.

### 3.4 Cost concentration / outlier risk
- The Accra–Kumasi Expressway alone (~$4bn estimated) represents close to 40% of the programme's
  entire headline value, using a distinct financing structure (a 50-year build-and-manage SPV)
  from the rest of the programme. A delay, cost overrule, or financing failure on this single
  project would disproportionately affect any programme-wide completion or spending statistic —
  Phase 5's reporting should isolate it rather than blend it into aggregate figures.

### 3.5 Maintenance (forward risk, not yet observable)
- No source in Phases 1–3 addressed post-completion maintenance funding or planning for Big Push
  specifically. Given that a documented driver of Ghana's underlying road-sector problem is
  exactly this — new construction without sustained maintenance funding — this is flagged as a
  significant gap in the evidence base itself, not just a programme risk: the final report should
  say plainly that no maintenance-funding plan for completed Big Push assets was found, rather
  than assume one exists.

## 4. Illustrative cost-benefit framing (scoped, not computed)

Per `methodology.md` §2.5, full cost-benefit analysis is only attempted for 2–3 flagship,
well-documented corridors, and even there, framed as illustrative rather than a definitive NPV/IRR
calculation, because per-project cost and benefit data of research-grade quality doesn't yet exist
for any Big Push project.

**What can be said now, for the three best-documented projects:**

| Project | Known cost side | Known benefit side | What's missing for a real CBA |
|---|---|---|---|
| Accra–Kumasi Expressway | ~$4bn estimated total; $1.7bn secured; 176km; 50-year SPV | None disclosed yet (still in feasibility as of mid-2026) | Toll revenue projections, traffic volume forecasts, travel-time savings estimates, construction cost breakdown |
| GMACP (feeder roads) | $523m total ($500m IDA + $23m GoG) | 550,000+ beneficiaries claimed, ~25,000 short-term jobs, >1,000km rehabilitated across 9 regions | Per-km cost, expected agricultural output/market-access gain, baseline (pre-project) travel times and farm-gate prices to compare against |
| Dambai Bridge (Oti River) | Contract value not disclosed; 1.49km + 6.8km approach roads | Connects Oti Region to the northern sector — a stated strategic/access rationale, not a quantified one | Contract value, current ferry/detour costs being replaced, projected traffic volume |

**Honest conclusion for this section:** a genuine cost-benefit analysis is not possible yet for any
Big Push project with public information found in this research. The World Bank's GMACP project
appraisal document (not directly fetchable here, but referenced in the press release) is the most
likely single source to contain real project-appraisal figures (expected rate of return, etc.) —
retrieving it directly is a priority for Phase 5 or a future session with working `WebFetch`/
document access.

## 5. What Phase 4 could not do, and why

- **No employment multiplier was estimated from this programme's own data** — none exists to
  estimate from (no Ghana Big Push input-output table is public). Any employment-effect claim in
  the final report will cite external literature explicitly, not present a number as if derived
  from this project's own analysis.
- **No debt-sustainability re-estimation was attempted** — this would require the IMF's full DSA
  model inputs, which are not accessible via `WebSearch` alone.
- **No project-level cost-benefit ratio was computed** — see §4. The table above is the honest
  ceiling of what's currently knowable, not a placeholder for numbers to be filled in later without
  new source access.
