# Ghana's Big Push Infrastructure Programme — Executive Summary

*For the full analysis with sources and caveats, see `full_report.md`. This is the short version.*

## What it is

Big Push is the Ghanaian government's (under President Mahama, since January 2025) major roads
and bridges programme, covering all 16 regions. It's been described as a $10 billion, multi-year
commitment, aimed at fixing a road network that's estimated to have a $1.5 billion annual
funding gap. The 2026 budget alone sets aside GH₵30–30.8 billion for it — roughly double the 2025
figure.

## The headline problem: nobody agrees on the basic numbers

How many projects is Big Push? Depending on which government announcement you read and when, the
answer has been 32, 49, 50, 72, 77, 87, or 140. How much is it worth in total? Somewhere between
roughly GH₵43 billion (summing the announced annual budgets) and GH₵110 billion (the figure a
professional engineers' body used when asking for an independent audit). Neither of these gaps
has an obvious innocent explanation, and neither is resolved in this report — they're documented
as open questions, because that's what the evidence supports.

## What's actually going well

- The Ministry of Roads and Highways runs its own public project tracker with completion
  rankings — a real, working piece of transparency infrastructure, whatever else is contested.
- More than 90% of contracts are reported as competitively tendered (the rest via single-source
  or restricted methods, which is itself the subject of dispute — see below).
- A separate $500 million World Bank-financed project (feeder roads across 9 regions) is
  confirmed and appears to be proceeding on its own track.

## What's contested, and by whom

- The **Ghana Institution of Engineering** formally asked the Auditor-General to independently
  audit GH₵110 billion in spending (April 2026). No audit has been completed yet.
- The **Parliamentary Minority** is demanding full disclosure of contract details — who the
  contractors are, what each is building, and the cost per kilometre.
- **Government** maintains that procurement was handled properly and that funding is domestic, no
  external debt involved — a claim in tension with the confirmed $500m World Bank credit discussed
  in the same period.
- Workers at the **Ghana Highway Authority itself** have publicly warned that old equipment and
  poor logistics could hold the programme back.

## What we genuinely don't know yet

Whether Big Push is actually working — creating jobs, cutting travel times, opening up markets for
farmers — can't be answered yet. Most projects are still early in construction (one closely
tracked bridge was 6% complete as of mid-2026). Any claim about the programme's economic benefits
right now is a prediction based on how infrastructure spending is supposed to work, not something
anyone has actually measured. This report is explicit about that distinction throughout, rather
than presenting hoped-for benefits as achieved ones.

## The single biggest fiscal risk worth watching

One project — the Accra–Kumasi Expressway — is estimated at about $4 billion, close to 40% of the
entire programme's headline value, and is being financed through a 50-year build-and-operate deal
structure unlike the rest of the programme. If that one project runs into trouble, it will
distort almost any summary statistic about how Big Push as a whole is performing.

## What would make this a success

1. One official, dated project list, so the count stops changing depending on who's asked and
   when.
2. The audit the engineers' body asked for, actually completed and published.
3. A clear answer on whether the World Bank money counts as part of Big Push or not.
4. A public maintenance plan for finished roads — the thing that determined whether Ghana's roads
   stayed good after previous infrastructure pushes, and something no source in this research
   mentioned having a plan for yet.
5. Real before-and-after measurement (travel time, market access, jobs) once the first roads are
   actually finished — so five years from now, "did it work" has an actual answer instead of a
   guess.
