# Ghana's Big Push Infrastructure Programme: What We Actually Know

*A plain-language breakdown of the full research report on Ghana's flagship $10 billion roads and
bridges programme. For the full analysis with sources and caveats, see `full_report.md`; this is
the short version.*

---

## Theme 1: The numbers don't add up (yet)

Big Push is the Mahama administration's flagship infrastructure programme, launched to close an
estimated $1.5 billion annual road financing gap. Annual budget allocations grew from about
GH₵13.9 billion in 2025 to roughly GH₵30.8 billion in 2026.

But even the basic facts are disputed. Across official channels, the total number of projects has
been reported as 32, 49, 50, 72, 77, 87, and 140, seven different figures for the same programme,
depending on the date, the scope, and who is speaking. The total value of the programme is just as
unsettled: government figures point to around GH₵43 billion committed so far, while civil society
groups cite a much larger GH₵110 billion scale, the figure the Ghana Institution of Engineering
used when formally asking the Auditor-General for an independent audit (April 2026).

None of this is resolved yet. What is confirmed is that no completed independent audit exists, and
no figure in this report can be labelled "actually spent." Every number is either announced,
budgeted, or claimed.

A separate, smaller programme worth naming here: a confirmed $500 million World Bank-financed
project (feeder roads across nine regions, the Ghana Market Access and Connectivity Project) is
proceeding on its own track. Whether it counts as part of Big Push or sits alongside it is itself
unresolved, and it sits awkwardly next to government's claim that Big Push funding is purely
domestic with no external debt involved.

**Bottom line:** Ghana has a real, functioning project tracker (the Ministry of Roads and Highways
runs one, with public completion rankings) and active independent scrutiny, but not yet a single,
trusted source of truth for how big Big Push really is.

## Theme 2: Procurement is where the real fight is happening

Government sources say more than 90% of Big Push contracts were competitively tendered. But the
exact breakdown of the remaining contracts (single source versus restricted tender) is itself
contested, with different reports giving different splits.

A government-linked investigation found no procurement abuse. At the same time, the Ghana
Institution of Engineering formally petitioned the Auditor-General for an independent technical
audit, and the Parliamentary Minority is demanding full contract-by-contract disclosure: who got
each contract, what it covers, and the cost per kilometre. Separately, workers at the Ghana Highway
Authority itself have publicly warned that old equipment and poor logistics could hold the
programme back, a capacity concern distinct from the funding and disclosure disputes.

One structural gap stands out: the National Roads Authority Act, passed in 2024 specifically to
strengthen independent oversight, has reportedly not yet been put into operation.

**Bottom line:** this is an active, attributed dispute rather than a settled question. The tools
for stronger oversight already exist in law; they just aren't being used yet.

## Theme 3: We can't yet measure whether it's working

This is the most important caveat in the whole report. Most Big Push projects are still early in
construction. The most closely tracked bridge was only 6% physically complete as of June 2026.
That means there is no before-and-after data yet on travel time, market access, or local
employment for any Big Push corridor.

So when anyone claims Big Push is already boosting jobs, trade, or incomes, that claim is a
projection from a theory of change, not a measured result. The evidence base is strong for inputs
(money allocated) and activities (contracts signed), thin for outputs (only 17 of 70-plus projects
have any public completion data), and essentially empty for outcomes and impact.

There's also a data gap on regional equity. Officially, the programme covers all 16 regions. But
the visible project sample is small and skewed toward whatever got news coverage, not necessarily
where the money and poverty need actually are. Rather than manufacture a misleading inequality
statistic from incomplete data, the report deliberately leaves this question open pending direct
access to the full project tracker.

**Bottom line:** it is too early to say whether Big Push is delivering results. Anyone claiming
otherwise, in either direction, is speculating.

### One project worth watching closely

A single project, the Accra-Kumasi Expressway, is estimated at about $4 billion, close to 40% of
the entire programme's headline value, and is being financed through a 50-year build-and-operate
deal structure unlike the rest of the programme. If that one project runs into trouble, it will
distort almost any summary statistic about how Big Push as a whole is performing, simply because
of its size relative to everything else in the programme.

## Theme 4: What would actually fix this

The report closes with a practical, non-partisan roadmap, building on institutions Ghana already
has rather than proposing something new:

1. **One official project list.** MRH and the National Development Planning Commission (NDPC)
   should jointly publish a single, dated project register, ending the situation where the public
   has to reconstruct the count from scattered announcements.
2. **Quarterly financial transparency.** Publish approved, released, and spent figures together,
   every quarter, not just approved amounts.
3. **Clarify GMACP's status.** State plainly whether the $500 million World Bank-financed GMACP
   project counts as part of Big Push or sits separately.
4. **Commission and publish an independent audit**, acting on the Ghana Institution of
   Engineering's pending petition.
5. **Activate the National Roads Authority Act (2024)**, the independent oversight body that
   already exists in law but hasn't been operationalised.
6. **Give NDPC real authority** to enforce monitoring and evaluation standards across ministries,
   described in the report as the single highest-leverage institutional change available.
7. **Require a maintenance funding plan** for every project before or at completion, closing a gap
   the research found completely undocumented, and the thing that determined whether Ghana's roads
   stayed good after previous infrastructure pushes.
8. **Start baseline surveys now** on travel time, market access, and employment, so that in a year
   or two Ghana can actually measure Big Push's impact instead of just projecting it.

**Bottom line:** none of these fixes require new invention. They require publishing what already
exists, activating laws already passed, and starting measurement before it's too late to have a
baseline.
