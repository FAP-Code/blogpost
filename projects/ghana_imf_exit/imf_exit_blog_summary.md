# Ghana's IMF Exit: What We Actually Know

*A plain-language breakdown of the full research report on Ghana's exit from its $3 billion IMF
Extended Credit Facility. For the full analysis with sources and caveats, see
`imf_exit_full_report.md`; this is the short version.*

---

## Theme 1: The stabilization is real, but "exit" is doing more work than it should

Ghana completed the sixth and final review of its 39-month, $3 billion IMF programme on July 27,
2026, releasing the last disbursement and bringing total support to the full $3 billion approved
back in May 2023. Inflation fell from above 20% in 2024 to roughly 5.3 to 5.4% by mid-2026 (one
lower figure of 3.3 to 3.4% is also reported and not yet reconciled with the rest). Reserves rose
to $14.5 billion, and both Fitch and S&P upgraded Ghana's credit rating in 2026.

But "exit" undersells what actually happened. Ghana didn't walk away from the IMF, it moved into a
36-month Policy Coordination Instrument, a lighter, non-financing form of continued Fund oversight.
That is a graduation, not a goodbye.

**Bottom line:** the macro numbers are genuinely strong and independently confirmed. Whether "exit"
means what it sounds like is a separate question, and the honest answer is: not quite.

---

## Theme 2: The debt story depends entirely on which number you're looking at

This is the single most confusing part of the whole picture. Ghana's own central bank reports debt
at 45.1% of GDP as of May 2026, down from a peak above 66%, a real and dramatic improvement. But
the IMF's own projections, published in the same review that just concluded the programme, show
that same ratio climbing back up to 53% by the end of 2026 and 55% by 2028. A separate law commits
Ghana to a 45%-of-GDP debt ceiling, but not until 2034, eight years from now.

Three different numbers, three different meanings: a current snapshot, a rising near-term
projection, and a distant future target. Public commentary regularly treats them as if they're the
same fact. They aren't.

**Bottom line:** debt-to-GDP fell dramatically and is genuinely projected to rise again, both at
the same time, according to the very institution that just signed off on the programme's success.

---

## Theme 3: Diversification is real but still small, and job creation is a pipeline, not a payroll

Non-traditional exports (cashew, shea, processed cocoa, and others) grew a genuine 30.7% in 2025 to
$5 billion, solid, independently reported evidence of real diversification. But traditional exports
(gold, oil, cocoa beans) still make up roughly 84% of everything Ghana sells abroad. This is
diversification from a small base, not yet a different kind of economy.

The flagship job programmes tell a similar story. The 24-Hour Economy policy reports $5.5 billion
in signed investment agreements and a target of 200,000 jobs in 2026. One Million Coders reports
roughly 140,000 registrations and somewhere between 12,000 and 28,000 people who've completed at
least some training (sources don't agree on the exact figure or what exactly counts). Both are
real, but both are pipelines, agreements signed, people enrolled, not verified counts of people
now employed because of these programmes. Even the government's own 24-Hour Economy Secretariat
says its progress should be judged by investments mobilized and jobs expected, language that
describes a plan in motion, not a result achieved.

**Bottom line:** there is genuine, sourced evidence of momentum on exports and investment. There is
not yet independently verified evidence that the job-creation programmes have produced the jobs
being announced. That's not the same as evidence they're failing, it's simply too early to measure.

---

## Theme 4: Ghana has been here before, twice, and both times it didn't last

This is the historical pattern the whole research brief was built to test. In 2006, Ghana completed
a HIPC-era debt relief programme and issued its first Eurobond in 2007, celebrated then much like
this exit is being celebrated now. Fiscal discipline relaxed afterward, and by 2009 the budget
deficit had reached 14.5% of GDP, forcing a new IMF programme.

It happened again more recently. Ghana's 2015 to 2019 IMF programme also achieved real
stabilization, inflation nearly halved, a banking-sector clean-up completed. Ghana then graduated
to lighter monitoring in 2019. Within three years, "structural rigidities left the country
completely exposed to the macro-shocks of 2020 to 2022," culminating in the debt crisis and default
that led directly to the current 2023 programme.

Twice now, a successful IMF exit has been followed, within roughly three years, by a slide back
into crisis. That's not proof it will happen a third time. But it's also exactly the risk the IMF's
own current review names explicitly: thin fiscal margins, heavy debt-service and wage costs, and
political pressure to spend more as elections approach.

**Bottom line:** the pattern is real and well-documented. Whether 2026 breaks it can only honestly
be judged around 2028 to 2029, the same interval it took the last two cycles to turn.
