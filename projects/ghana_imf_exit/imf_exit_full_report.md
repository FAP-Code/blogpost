# Ghana's Exit from the IMF Extended Credit Facility: Stabilization or Transformation?

**Prepared:** 2026-08-03, via `WebSearch` only in a single working session. **This is a first
evidence pass, not a final audited verdict.** Every figure below carries a verification status tag
(`officially_announced`, `independently_verified`, `political_claim`, or `projection_not_measured`)
and a source with an access date. Where two credible sources disagree on the same fact, both
numbers are shown, not silently reconciled. See the Data Limitations and Discrepancy Log sections
before citing any figure from this report elsewhere.

**Core finding, stated plainly:** Ghana's macro stabilization under the 2023 to 2026 ECF is real
and independently corroborated (falling inflation, rebuilt reserves, two credit rating upgrades).
Whether it adds up to genuine structural transformation, rather than a repeat of Ghana's 2006 and
2019 post-programme relapses, is not answerable yet from the evidence available in mid-2026. The
report says so explicitly rather than forcing a verdict the data doesn't support.

---

## 1. Executive summary

Ghana concluded the sixth and final review of its $3 billion, 39-month Extended Credit Facility
(ECF) arrangement with the IMF Executive Board on 2026-07-27, releasing a final disbursement of
about $371 million and bringing total disbursements to the full $3 billion approved in May 2023.
Rather than a clean exit, Ghana immediately moved into a 36-month Policy Coordination Instrument
(PCI), a non-financing IMF arrangement that keeps Fund monitoring in place without further loans.
That detail matters for how "exit" should be read: this is a graduation to a lighter form of IMF
engagement, not an end to it.

The headline numbers are genuinely strong and come from multiple independent sources: inflation
fell from above 20% in 2024 to roughly 5.3 to 5.4% by mid-2026 (with one lower figure of 3.3 to
3.4% also reported and not yet reconciled, see the Discrepancy Log), reserves rose to $14.5 billion
(5.8 months of import cover) by February 2026, GDP growth accelerated to 6.4% year-on-year in
2026Q1, and Fitch and S&P both upgraded Ghana's sovereign credit rating in 2026. Moody's, notably,
has not moved as far, leaving Ghana at Caa1, a reminder that "the agencies upgraded Ghana" is not
a fully unanimous statement.

The debt picture is the most contested part of the whole story. Ghana's own Bank of Ghana reports
debt at 45.1% of GDP as of May 2026, a dramatic fall from a peak above 66%. But the IMF's own
projections, published in the same review that concluded the programme, show that ratio rising
again to 53% by the end of 2026 and 55% by 2028. A separate, legislated debt anchor commits Ghana
to bring debt down to 45% of GDP, but not until 2034. Reporting that treats "45%" as a single,
settled, permanent achievement is conflating three different things: a snapshot, a rising
projection, and a target eight years away.

On structural transformation specifically: non-traditional exports (cashew, shea, processed cocoa
products, and others) grew a genuinely strong 30.7% in 2025 to $5.0 billion, an area with real,
independently reported momentum. But traditional exports (gold, oil, cocoa beans) still account for
roughly 84% of merchandise exports, so this is diversification from a small base, not yet a change
in the country's basic export structure. Flagship job-creation programmes (the 24-Hour Economy
policy, One Million Coders) report pipeline commitments, investment agreements, and training
enrollments, not verified, audited employment counts. It is too early, by the government's own
programme timelines, for job creation to be measured rather than projected.

Ghana has been here before. A 2006 HIPC-era exit and 2007 Eurobond was followed by fiscal
relaxation and a 14.5%-of-GDP deficit by 2009, forcing a new IMF programme. A 2015 to 2019 ECF exit
was followed by "structural rigidities [that] left the country completely exposed to the
macro-shocks of 2020 to 2022," culminating in the debt crisis that produced the current programme.
Both of Ghana's last two programme exits were followed by a return to crisis within roughly three
years. Whether 2026 breaks that pattern is not something this report, or anyone, can honestly
answer yet.

## 2. Background: what the ECF was and how it ends

The IMF Executive Board approved a 36-month, SDR 2.242 billion (about $3 billion) ECF arrangement
for Ghana on 2023-05-17, building on the government's Post-COVID-19 Programme for Economic Growth
(PC-PEG). It was Ghana's response to the 2022 financial crisis: loss of market access, a collapsing
cedi, surging inflation, and unsustainable debt that forced a domestic and external debt
restructuring. The programme was later extended by three months to 39 months total. `officially_announced`

Disbursement proceeded in tranches tied to periodic reviews:

| Tranche | Amount | Date | Milestone |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1st | $600m | 2023-05-17 | Board approval |
| 2nd | $600m | 2024-01-19 | 1st review complete |
| 3rd | $360m | 2024-07 | Programme targets met |
| 4th | $360m | 2024-12 | 3rd review complete |
| 5th | $385m | 2025-12-17 | 5th review complete |
| 6th (final) | ~$371m (SDR 265.9m) | 2026-07-27 | 6th and final review complete |

One source describes a "6th tranche" of $360m released in "early 2026" for meeting "revised fiscal
and governance benchmarks," which does not cleanly match the $371m final disbursement dated
2026-07-27 in the IMF's own press release. This is logged, not resolved, in the Discrepancy Log
below (item 7). `officially_announced` (tranche schedule) / unresolved (early-2026 $360m figure)

**How the exit actually works:** this is not an automatic expiry. The Executive Board formally
completed the sixth review, concluded the 2026 Article IV Consultation, and separately reviewed
Ghana's request for a 36-month Policy Coordination Instrument (PCI), all on 2026-07-27. The PCI is
a non-financing instrument used for countries "no longer fac[ing] balance of payment challenges,"
meaning Ghana chose continued, reform-focused IMF engagement rather than a clean break. Some
domestic reporting refers to an "April 2026" or "May 2026" exit date; the IMF's own press release
dating the Board's completed review is 2026-07-27 and is treated here as the authoritative date
(Tier 1/2 source). `officially_announced` / `independently_verified`

## 4. Historical comparison: has Ghana been here before?

Ghana's relationship with the IMF long predates this programme. Sources disagree on the exact
count (see Discrepancy Log item 1), but this is somewhere around Ghana's 16th to 17th IMF
arrangement since the 1980s, including four separate arrangements between 1983 and 1988 under the
Rawlings-era structural adjustment programmes, continuous engagement through 1995 to 2002, a
mid-2000s HIPC-era programme, the 2015 to 2019 ECF, and the current 2023 to 2026 ECF.

Two prior post-programme periods are directly relevant to the "stabilization without
transformation" question this report was commissioned to investigate:

**2006 to 2009.** Ghana completed the HIPC initiative and issued its first sovereign Eurobond in
2007, both read at the time as milestones of graduation from aid dependency. Fiscal relaxation
followed, without new structural guardrails, and by 2009 the budget deficit had reached 14.5% of
GDP, forcing a return to an IMF programme. `independently_verified`

**2015 to 2019.** Ghana's ECF (approved 2015-04-03, SDR 664.2m, about $918m, roughly one-third the
size of the current programme) ran through an extension to 2019-04-02. At approval, inflation stood
near 17% (2014); by the programme's last review in early 2019, inflation had fallen to about 9%,
alongside fiscal consolidation and a banking-sector clean-up. Ghana then "graduat[ed] to a
monitoring phase in 2019," but "structural rigidities left the country completely exposed to the
macro-shocks of 2020 to 2022, culminating in an avoidable debt crisis," the crisis that produced
the 2022 default and the 2023 ECF now concluding. `independently_verified`

**What this comparison actually supports:** both of Ghana's last two programme exits were followed,
within roughly three years, by a return to fiscal crisis and a new IMF programme. That is a real,
citable pattern, not an inevitability. It does not prove the current exit will repeat it; the IMF
itself explicitly names "avoiding past policy slippages...including recurring cycles of fiscal
imbalances, rising debt, weak buffers, and reform reversals" as the central risk to watch, which is
this report's own conclusion stated in the Fund's own words. `independently_verified`

## 5. Structural transformation: the evidence, sorted by how solid it is

### 5.1 Where the evidence is genuinely strong: non-traditional exports

Ghana's non-traditional exports (cashew, shea, processed cocoa products, bananas, yams, and others)
reached $5.006 billion in 2025, up 30.7% from a prior-year figure reported as either $3.08bn or
$3.28bn depending on the source (a minor, likely scope-related discrepancy, see Discrepancy Log
item 5). Processed and semi-processed products drove most of the growth (+52.78% to $3.09bn, 83.5%
of NTE earnings), and agricultural exports specifically grew 37.82% (cashew, shea, and a striking
559% jump in yam exports). This data comes from GEPA's own annual statistics report, a genuine
Tier 1 primary source, not a political claim. `independently_verified`

The important caveat: non-traditional exports are still only about 16% of total merchandise
exports. Gold, oil, and cocoa beans together were over 70% of exports as recently as 2023 and
remain dominant. This is real diversification, from a small base, not yet a changed export
structure at the aggregate level. This finding directly parallels this site's own "Beyond Cocoa"
research series on the same underlying question for Ghana's agricultural exports specifically.

### 5.2 Where the evidence is a genuine but early positive signal: foreign direct investment

FDI inflows reached $2.61 billion in 2025 (253 projects), roughly four times the $652 million
recorded in 2024. This is one year of data, and one caveat is worth flagging explicitly: the
largest single source by value was the Cayman Islands ($500m), a jurisdiction commonly used as a
holding-company or tax domicile for capital that may originate elsewhere, a standard caution with
FDI statistics generally, not specific evidence of anything improper here. China led by project
count (70 projects, $486m). One year of a rebound, after a crisis-driven collapse, is a positive
signal, not yet a trend. `officially_announced` (GIPC)

### 5.3 Where the evidence is a pipeline, not a measured outcome: job creation programmes

The 24-Hour Economy policy (launched July 2025) and One Million Coders programme (pilot from 2025,
nationwide rollout from April 2026) are this administration's flagship structural-transformation
initiatives. Both report substantial pipeline activity: the 24-Hour Economy Secretariat cites $5.5
billion in signed Joint Development Agreements and 268 filling stations plus 33 manufacturers
operating under a multi-shift model as of mid-2026, working toward a stated target of 200,000 jobs
in 2026 and 1.7 million by 2028. One Million Coders reports 130 to 175 training centres (sources
disagree, see Discrepancy Log item 6), roughly 140,000 registrations, about 40,000 enrolled, and
between 12,000 and 28,000 people reported as having completed at least one module or course
(different sources use different completion metrics and dates, not necessarily contradictory but
not directly comparable either). `officially_announced`

Two things are worth being explicit about. First, the 24-Hour Economy Secretariat itself has said
its progress should be judged by "investments mobilised, production capacity created, exports
generated and jobs expected," language that describes a pipeline of commitments, not a headcount of
people currently employed. Second, One Million Coders is a training and skilling programme; even at
full enrollment, "trained" is not the same claim as "employed," and no source found in this
research verifies net employment outcomes attributable to either programme. **This report does not
find independently verified evidence that either programme has yet produced the job numbers being
cited in public messaging**, and states this as a data gap, not as evidence the programmes are
failing. `projection_not_measured`

## 6. Risk assessment: what independent and dissenting voices say

**The IMF's own review**, the same document that concludes the successful programme, is
unusually direct about the risks ahead: statutory payments, interest, and wages absorb roughly 70%
of total government expenditure, leaving "thin margins for error"; the 1.5%-of-GDP primary surplus
target is "achievable only under disciplined execution"; and the Fund names political pressure to
spend ahead of elections and an "ambitious" GH₵268 billion 2026 revenue target as specific,
concrete risks, not vague ones. The IMF's own debt projections (53% of GDP by end-2026, 55% by
2028) sit in tension with the "successful exit" framing used elsewhere in the same set of
communications. `independently_verified`

**Credit rating agencies are not unanimous.** Fitch and S&P both upgraded Ghana in 2026, citing
reserves, fiscal discipline, and the debt anchor. Moody's has not moved comparably, leaving Ghana
at Caa1. A claim that "the rating agencies upgraded Ghana" is accurate for two of three major
agencies, not all three. `officially_announced`

**Political dispute over credit, not just numbers.** The opposition NPP disputes the current
government's framing on two separate grounds: first, that the stabilization "began under the
previous NPP administration," with IMF negotiations, debt restructuring, and fiscal adjustment
already underway before the current administration took office in January 2025; second, on the
numbers themselves, specifically citing the gap between the Bank of Ghana's 45.1%-of-GDP debt
figure and the IMF's own 53% (end-2026) and 55% (2028) projections as evidence the government's
debt narrative is incomplete. The Minority has also called the 2026 budget "growthless." This
report treats the credit-attribution dispute as a political claim on both sides, not something
resolvable from economic data, while noting the debt-projection dispute is a genuine, sourced
factual tension (already covered in Section 3). `political_claim` (attribution dispute) /
`independently_verified` (the underlying debt-projection numbers themselves)

**A named "stabilization without transformation" framing already exists in Ghanaian commentary.**
At least one commentary explicitly uses this phrase to describe the current moment, while also
noting that "for millions of ordinary Ghanaians still struggling with high food prices,
unemployment, rising utility costs...the celebration appears disconnected from life on the ground."
This is the clearest evidence found in this research that the exact question this report was
commissioned to investigate is already a live, named debate inside Ghana, not one imported from
outside. `independently_verified` (that the debate exists) / the underlying "disconnected from life
on the ground" claim itself is `political_claim` / anecdotal, not measured by any indicator this
research located.

**This report did not locate a distinct, dedicated IMANI Africa commentary on this specific 2026
ECF exit** in this research pass, unlike the comparable Ghana Big Push project, where IMANI had a
clearly citable position. This is recorded as a research gap, not as evidence IMANI has no view.

## 7. Discrepancy log

Every entry below is a place two or more credible sources disagree on a fact that should, in
principle, have one answer. None are resolved by picking a winner; each is flagged for a future
pass with direct primary-source access.

1. **Number of prior IMF arrangements.** One source states this is Ghana's 17th approach to the
   IMF; another states the 2015 programme was the 16th. Unresolved.
2. **Debt-to-GDP, 2026.** 45.1% (Bank of Ghana, May 2026) vs. 42.2% (a separate source, exact date
   unclear) vs. the IMF's own 53% (end-2026) and 55% (2028) projections vs. the 45%-by-2034
   legislated anchor (a target, not a current level). These are not all measuring the same thing,
   but public commentary frequently treats them as interchangeable. Unresolved as stated; the
   distinction between snapshot, projection, and 2034 target is explained in Section 3.
3. **Inflation, 2026.** 5.3 to 5.4% (IMF, June 2026 / end-2025) vs. 3.3 to 3.4% (April 2026, a
   different source). A roughly two-percentage-point gap for what should be close reporting
   periods. Unresolved.
4. **Primary fiscal balance.** 2.1% of GDP surplus (undated within 2026) vs. 1.2% of GDP (March
   2026, a partial-year figure) vs. 1.5% (the 2025/2026 budget target, not an outturn at all).
   Unresolved; likely reflects different reporting periods being cited without that context.
5. **Non-traditional export base year (2024).** $3.08bn vs. $3.28bn cited across sources for what
   should be the same figure. Minor; possibly a scope difference (e.g., a "top 10" subset vs. the
   full total). Unresolved.
6. **One Million Coders infrastructure and completions.** 130 vs. 175 training centres cited
   across different dates in 2026 (may reflect genuine programme growth rather than a true
   conflict); "over 12,000 completed courses" (May 2026) vs. "more than 28,000 completed at least
   one module" (a later report) may describe different metrics rather than contradictory ones, but
   no source reconciles them explicitly. Unresolved.
7. **Sixth ECF tranche amount.** One source describes a "$360m" sixth tranche released in "early
   2026" for meeting "revised fiscal and governance benchmarks"; the IMF's own press release dates
   the sixth and final review's disbursement at approximately $371m (SDR 265.9m) on 2026-07-27.
   These may be the same disbursement misdescribed by an intermediate source, or two genuinely
   different events. Unresolved.
8. **Exact "exit" date.** Some domestic reporting refers to an April or May 2026 exit; the IMF's
   own Executive Board completion date for the sixth and final review is 2026-07-27. This report
   treats the IMF's dated press release as authoritative and flags the earlier dates as likely
   referring to anticipated timing discussed before the Board's actual decision.

## 8. Data limitations

This research was conducted using `WebSearch` only, in a single working session, on 2026-08-03.
`WebFetch` (direct page retrieval) returned HTTP 403 for every domain tested, including imf.org,
confirming the same network-layer restriction documented in this repository's Ghana Big Push
project (see that project's `methodology.md` §2.7). Every figure in this report is therefore a
search-engine synthesis of an underlying primary or secondary source, not a verbatim pull from the
original document. Treat every number here as provisional until checked directly against its cited
primary source. No dataset file (CSV) or reproducible analysis code accompanies this report, unlike
the Big Push project, because the brief called for a research report and blog summary rather than a
structured dataset; this could be added in a future pass if useful.

## 9. Unanswered questions and what would resolve them

1. **Does debt-to-GDP actually stabilize, or rise to the IMF's own projected 53 to 55%?** Resolves
   with 2026 to 2028 fiscal-year outturn data, not available until roughly 2027 to 2029.
2. **Do the 24-Hour Economy and One Million Coders programmes produce verified net employment,**
   as opposed to signed agreements, training enrollments, and projected job counts? Resolves with
   an independent labour-force survey (Ghana Statistical Service) tied to specific programme
   beneficiaries, not yet conducted or at least not yet found in this research.
3. **Is non-traditional export growth durable, or a one-year base-effect and commodity-price
   story?** Resolves with at least two to three more years of GEPA annual reports.
4. **Which inflation and primary-balance figures are correct for 2026?** Resolves with a single
   reconciled release from the Bank of Ghana and Ministry of Finance, ideally jointly, explaining
   the reporting-period differences behind the discrepancies logged above.
5. **Does this cycle avoid the fate of the 2006 and 2019 exits?** By the pattern of the prior two
   relapses (each roughly three years post-exit), this becomes answerable only around 2028 to 2029.
   Nothing in the current evidence proves either outcome; the IMF's own review names the exact risk
   (fiscal slippage, election-year spending pressure) that produced the prior two relapses as the
   risk to watch this time.

## 10. Policy recommendations

Each recommendation names a responsible actor and a concrete definition of "done," in the same
format as this repository's Ghana Big Push report.

1. **Reconcile the debt-to-GDP figures.** Actor: Bank of Ghana and Ministry of Finance, jointly.
   Done: a single published note explaining the gap between the ~45% current snapshot and the
   IMF's own 53% (end-2026) and 55% (2028) projections, including which assumptions drive the
   difference.
2. **Verify job-creation claims independently.** Actor: Ghana Statistical Service, in coordination
   with the 24-Hour Economy Secretariat and Youth Employment Agency. Done: an independently
   conducted headcount or labour-force survey of programme beneficiaries, published on a fixed
   schedule, distinct from the Secretariats' own pipeline/agreement figures.
3. **Publish annual Fiscal Responsibility Framework compliance reports.** Actor: Ministry of
   Finance. Done: a public annual report showing actual primary balance, revenue, and expenditure
   outturns against the Framework's numeric targets, not just budget-stage targets.
4. **Commission an independent comparative analysis of this cycle against the 2006 to 2009 and
   2019 to 2022 relapses.** Actor: National Development Planning Commission or an independent
   academic or think-tank body. Done: a published analysis naming the specific institutional
   differences (if any) between this exit and the prior two, since the IMF's own stated risk
   (fiscal slippage around elections) is the same risk that produced both prior relapses.
5. **Track non-traditional export growth over a multi-year window before calling diversification
   durable.** Actor: Ghana Export Promotion Authority (GEPA). Done: three consecutive annual
   reports showing sustained growth and a rising share of total exports, not a single strong year.
6. **Resolve the count of Ghana's historical IMF engagements.** Actor: Ministry of Finance or Bank
   of Ghana archives. Done: one official, footnoted historical timeline resolving the 16-vs-17
   discrepancy, a small but telling test of basic institutional record-keeping.
