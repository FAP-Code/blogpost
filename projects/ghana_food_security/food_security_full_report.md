# Food Security and the Food Glut Paradox in Ghana

**Prepared:** 2026-08-03, via `WebSearch` only in a single working session. Every figure carries a
verification status tag (`officially_confirmed`, `independently_verified`, `estimated`, or
`disputed`) and a source with an access date. Where sources disagree, both are shown; see the
Discrepancy Log before citing any figure from this report elsewhere.

**Core finding, stated plainly:** Ghana's food glut paradox is not one problem, it is at least two
different problems that happen to sit inside the same national food system. In the south (Ashanti,
Bono, Bono East), farmers periodically cannot sell tomatoes, maize, and onions at a profitable
price, largely a storage, market-access, and cross-border trade problem. In the north, food
insecurity is chronic, structural, and linked to poverty and now conflict-driven refugee inflows
from Burkina Faso, not primarily a local glut-and-waste problem. A 2025 continental food-security
monitor states the connecting insight precisely: national harvests are surging while millions of
Ghanaians still cannot afford enough food, meaning availability is no longer the binding constraint
nationally, affordability and access are. Treating these as one undifferentiated crisis, as this
report was explicitly asked not to do, would misdiagnose all three.

---

## 1. Executive summary

Ghana's food system currently produces two visible symptoms of the same underlying structural
weakness. First, seasonal gluts: tomato farmers in Asante Akyem Agogo (Ashanti Region) and Techiman
North (Bono East) reported bumper harvests left unsold or heavily discounted in 2026, maize farmers
in the Bono Region describe a grain glut threatening their investments, and a November 2025
continental monitor found Ghanaian farmers holding 1.2 million metric tonnes of unsold grain
nationally, including roughly US$330 million (about GH₵5 billion) of unsold paddy rice alone.
`independently_verified`

Second, and simultaneously, food insecurity remains widespread and by some measures worsening.
The same November 2025 report found 23.96% of Ghanaians, about 8 million people, facing
Insufficient Food Consumption, a 55.6% rise since 2023. A separate report citing Ghana Statistical
Service data puts the figure at 12.5 million people facing hunger; a third figure describes food
insecurity at emergency or crisis levels reaching "over 2 million people," the highest since 2017.
These are not necessarily contradictory, they likely measure different things (see Discrepancy Log
item 1), but no single source reconciles them, and this report does not pick one as "the" number.
`independently_verified` (that multiple such figures exist) / the reconciliation between them is
`disputed`

The most useful single sentence explaining the paradox comes directly from that November 2025
monitor: national harvests are surging, but the "last mile" of affordability is failing, meaning
production is not the primary constraint anymore, the ability of a household's income to reach that
food is. `independently_verified`

Regionally, the two stories diverge sharply. Northern and Transition Zone regions hold 56% of all
food-insecure people and effectively all Emergency-level cases, while containing only 28% of
Ghana's population, a chronic, structural pattern compounded by refugee inflows from conflict in
Burkina Faso. The glut stories, by contrast, are concentrated in the relatively more productive
south, a market and infrastructure failure layered on top of otherwise successful farming, not a
scarcity problem. `independently_verified`

Structurally, the report finds no single cause. Post-harvest losses are large but genuinely
disputed in size (maize estimates alone range from 7.5% to 53.8% across different studies).
Storage technology that works exists and has been piloted (a cold-storage system cut onion losses
from 30% to 5% in a field trial), but has not been scaled. A flagship national tomato-processing
factory has sat stalled for decades despite repeated calls to revive it. And the institution built
specifically to buy up surplus and stabilize prices, the National Food Buffer Stock Company
(NAFCO), was itself the subject of a documented 2025 corruption investigation. `independently_verified`

## 2. Background: stating the paradox clearly

Ghana's food system regularly produces both extremes described in this report's brief: seasonal
gluts of staple crops where farmers cannot recover their production costs, and persistent food
insecurity and food-price inflation for consumers, concentrated in northern Ghana and among
urban low-income households. `independently_verified`

Food inflation itself has been volatile through 2026: 2.30% year-on-year in March, then a rise to
2.2% in April and 3.3% in May, a renewed inflationary trend rather than a single stable figure.
`independently_verified`. This sits inside a food system where fertilizer prices were projected to
rise 38% in 2026 due to global supply disruptions linked to Middle East tensions, an input-cost
pressure that affects farmers regardless of whether their own crop faces a glut or a shortage.
`independently_verified`

The November 2025 AGRA Food Security Monitor is the single clearest statement of the paradox this
report investigates: production is up, informal cross-border trade channels that used to absorb
Ghana's surplus have been disrupted (military rule in Burkina Faso, policy changes in Nigeria), and
farmers are left holding grain they cannot sell, while millions of Ghanaian households simultaneously
cannot afford enough food. Both halves of that sentence are independently sourced; neither cancels
the other out. `independently_verified`

## 4. Documented glut cases and post-harvest losses

**Tomato, Ashanti Region (Asante Akyem Agogo), 2026:** farmers reported a bumper harvest leaving
large quantities at risk of spoilage due to limited market access and no functional storage.
`independently_verified`

**Tomato, Techiman North (Bono East Region), ongoing into 2026:** farmers specifically blame
unchecked imports of raw tomatoes from Burkina Faso for flooding local markets and leaving
domestically grown tomatoes unsold or heavily discounted, a cross-border competition dynamic
distinct from a pure storage problem. `independently_verified`. A government official (Eric Opoku)
publicly forecast a further tomato glut within three to four months as of a July 2026 statement.
`officially_confirmed` (that the statement was made) / the forecast itself is `estimated`

**Maize and grain, Bono Region, 2026:** farmers described an oversupply threatening their
investments, with abandoned storage facilities and collapsing prices. Reporting attributes this to
a combination of a government export ban on grains and continued lower-cost imports both weighing
on the same domestic price at once, a combination this research could not fully reconcile into a
single clear mechanism; see Discrepancy Log item 5. `independently_verified`

**Onion and tomato, Ashanti Region, September 2025:** the government purchased 1,000 boxes of
tomatoes and 2,000 bags of onions directly from farmers as an emergency response to oversupply, a
concrete, small-scale, ad hoc example of the state acting as buyer of last resort rather than a
systemic fix. `officially_confirmed`

**Post-harvest loss estimates, genuinely disputed rather than settled:**

| Crop | Estimate | Source basis | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tomato | ~30% (15,300 of 51,000 metric tonnes produced, one study) | specific field study | `estimated` |
| Tomato | 20 to 50% (broader range) | wider literature review | `estimated` |
| Maize | 7.5 to 53.8% (wide range across studies) | multiple studies | `disputed` |
| Maize | 14%, 38%, and 46% (individual point estimates) | separate individual studies | `disputed` |
| Onion | 30% in traditional storage, reduced to 5% with a CoolBot cold-storage system | field trial | `estimated` |

**The single most encouraging, concrete data point in this research** is the onion CoolBot trial:
it shows that appropriate, relatively low-cost cold-storage technology can cut losses by roughly
five-sixths in a controlled trial. The open question is why this hasn't been scaled nationally;
this report did not find a funded rollout plan, only the pilot result itself.

## 5. Structural causes: why gluts and scarcity coexist

This report does not attribute the paradox to a single cause. The evidence supports several
distinct, compounding mechanisms:

1. **Storage and cold-chain infrastructure is inadequate**, and where better technology has been
   piloted (the onion CoolBot trial), it works, suggesting the constraint is scaling and investment,
   not a lack of a technical answer. `estimated` (loss reduction figure) / `independently_verified` (that the gap exists)
2. **Cross-border trade disruption cuts both ways.** Regional informal trade channels that used to
   absorb Ghana's surplus grain were disrupted by military rule in Burkina Faso and policy changes
   in Nigeria, worsening the glut side. At the same time, imports (raw tomatoes from Burkina Faso in
   particular) are separately blamed for undercutting domestic producers even amid a domestic
   glut, meaning the same neighboring country's instability and trade patterns show up on both sides
   of the ledger. `independently_verified`
3. **Import dependence in rice and poultry sits in tension with a reported domestic glut.** Ghana's
   poultry self-sufficiency is estimated at under 5% (roughly 15,000 of 324,000 metric tonnes
   consumed produced domestically), and rice self-sufficiency was estimated at about 43% as of 2020,
   yet the same period's reporting describes a domestic paddy rice glut worth roughly US$330 million
   unsold. A plausible explanation, consumer preference for imported parboiled/milled rice over
   locally processed paddy, would reconcile this, but no source in this research pass directly
   confirmed that mechanism; it is offered here as a plausible inference, not a verified fact. See
   Discrepancy Log item 4. `estimated` (self-sufficiency figures) / the reconciling explanation is
   an inference, not independently confirmed
4. **Market information and structured-market gaps** are widely assumed to contribute (farmers not
   knowing prices in other regions or having a structured way to sell into them), but this research
   pass did not locate a specific, current, dated study evaluating Ghana's market information
   systems; this is recorded as a data gap in Section 9 rather than filled in with an assumption.

## 6. Policy response evaluation: promised versus delivered

**Planting for Food and Jobs (PFJ), original phase:** independently credited with real production
gains, maize yields rose about 67% and rice yields about 48% per hectare in 2017, and PFJ inputs
had generated roughly 1.2 million extra tonnes of cereal and legume output by 2019.
`independently_verified`. But the same body of research finds the programme's focus on primary
production (seeds, fertilizer, credit) over storage and value-addition created a "dependency
syndrome," beneficiaries relying on input support without investing in processing or storage
capacity. Read alongside this report's core paradox, PFJ appears to have helped increase the supply
side of the glut problem without a matching investment in the storage and processing side that
would have absorbed that extra supply, a specific, sourced, non-obvious causal link, not a general
criticism. `independently_verified`

**PFJ Phase II (PFJ 2.0):** the Ministry of Food and Agriculture states the programme has clear
strategic objectives to deliver food security and resilience within five years. This research did
not find an independent evaluation of PFJ 2.0's actual results to date; see Section 9.
`officially_confirmed` (that the stated objectives exist) / independent verification of results is
an open question

**National Food Buffer Stock Company (NAFCO):** mandated to reduce post-harvest losses and
stabilize food supply and prices, and to supply raw materials to agro-processors. In 2026 it
licensed 14 companies to procure food directly from farming communities for delivery to NAFCO
warehouses. `officially_confirmed`. However, an October 2025 investigative report documents a
corruption scandal at NAFCO specifically described as having crippled the institution's food
security function, a serious, sourced allegation this research could not independently verify
beyond the existence of the reporting itself, and whose resolution (if any) was not established in
this pass. This mirrors a pattern this site's research has now documented at more than one Ghanaian
institution built to manage a public resource (see the Big Push and IMF Exit projects elsewhere in
this repository), worth noting as a recurring institutional risk rather than treating any single
case as conclusive of a broader claim. `independently_verified` (that the allegation and reporting
exist) / the underlying allegations themselves are `disputed` pending any official resolution

**Pwalugu Tomato Factory:** built under Ghana's first president, Kwame Nkrumah, to process tomatoes
into puree and paste for local and export markets, it has remained stalled, abandoned, or
underperforming for decades. As recently as April 2025, the ICU (a workers' union) was still
publicly urging government to revive it. One industry expert has claimed the factory, if
operational, could process all of Ghana's tomato output within three months, a striking claim about
unused capacity, but a single expert's estimate, not an independently verified capacity figure.
`independently_verified` (that the factory is stalled and advocacy continues) / the capacity claim
is `estimated`, from a single source

**Warehouse receipt systems:** this research found only historical references (Bank of Ghana
support noted in older reporting, a 2023 piece on grain storage and warehousing) and no specific,
current, dated evaluation of an operating warehouse receipt system as of 2025 or 2026. This is
recorded as a genuine data gap, not evidence the system doesn't exist; see Section 9.

## 7. Regional disaggregation: two different problems, not one

**Northern Ghana (and the wider Transition Zone): a chronic, structural crisis.** These regions
hold 56% of all food-insecure Ghanaians and effectively all Emergency-level cases, on just 28% of
the national population, a concentration roughly double what population share alone would predict.
This is compounded by an influx of refugees fleeing conflict in Burkina Faso, placing additional
strain on already limited resources. This is a poverty, climate, and now conflict-linked story, not
primarily a story about local farmers being unable to sell a bumper harvest. `independently_verified`

**Southern Ghana (Ashanti, Bono, Bono East): a market and infrastructure failure story.** The
documented tomato, maize, and onion glut cases in this report are concentrated here, in relatively
more agriculturally productive areas. The mechanism is storage, market access, and cross-border
trade competition, not a lack of food being grown. `independently_verified`

**Why the distinction matters, concretely:** a policy response designed for the south's problem
(more cold storage, a functioning tomato factory, better market information) would do very little
for the north's problem (chronic poverty, conflict-driven displacement, climate vulnerability), and
vice versa. Treating "food insecurity" as one undifferentiated national crisis risks funding the
wrong fix in the wrong place. This report keeps the two separate throughout, as its brief required.

## 8. Discrepancy log

1. **Three different national food-insecurity headline figures**: 23.96% / about 8 million people
   (AGRA Food Security Monitor, Insufficient Food Consumption measure, November 2025) vs. 12.5
   million people (a separate report citing Ghana Statistical Service data, "hunger") vs. "over 2
   million people" at Emergency/crisis level (a third, narrower severity-based figure). No source
   found in this research reconciles these three into one consistent picture; they most likely use
   different definitions and thresholds. Unresolved.
2. **Tomato post-harvest loss**: a specific study figure (~30%, 15,300 of 51,000 metric tonnes) sits
   inside a broader cited range (20 to 50%) from the wider literature; not strictly contradictory,
   but risks being cited as "the" national figure when it is one study's result for a specific
   context. Flagged for clarity, not fully resolved.
3. **Maize post-harvest loss**: a genuinely wide, disputed range (7.5% to 53.8%), with individual
   point estimates of 14%, 38%, and 46% all appearing separately in the literature. Unresolved.
4. **Rice glut vs. rice import dependence**: Ghana is reported to hold a domestic paddy rice glut
   worth roughly US$330 million unsold, while separately having a rice self-sufficiency ratio of
   only about 43% (a 2020 figure) and remaining heavily import-dependent. A quality or preference
   mismatch between imported milled rice and local paddy is a plausible reconciling explanation but
   was not directly confirmed by any source in this research pass. Unresolved.
5. **Maize glut, export ban, and imports simultaneously blamed**: reporting attributes the same
   Bono Region maize price collapse to both a government export ban on grains and continued
   lower-cost imports, without a source clearly explaining how both mechanisms operate on the same
   crop at once. Unresolved.

## 9. Data limitations and unanswered questions

This research was conducted using `WebSearch` only, in a single working session, on 2026-08-03.
Based on the identical constraint documented in this repository's Ghana Big Push, IMF Exit, and
Fuel Pricing projects (a network-layer block on direct page retrieval), `WebFetch` is assumed
blocked here as well and treated as such; every figure in this report is a search-engine synthesis
of an underlying source, not a verbatim pull from an original document.

Unanswered questions, and what would resolve them:

1. **How do the three national food-insecurity figures (8 million, 12.5 million, "over 2 million")
   relate to one another?** Resolves with a single official Ghana Statistical Service or WFP
   methodology document defining and comparing these measures directly.
2. **Is a warehouse receipt system currently operating in Ghana, and at what scale?** This research
   found only stale references; resolves with a current NAFCO or Bank of Ghana status report.
3. **What has PFJ 2.0 actually achieved so far against its stated five-year objectives?** Resolves
   with an independent midpoint evaluation, not yet found in this research.
4. **Is there a funded plan to revive the Pwalugu Tomato Factory, and on what timeline?** Resolves
   with an official Ministry of Trade and Industry or Ministry of Food and Agriculture statement.
5. **What is the mechanism reconciling Ghana's reported rice glut with its continued heavy rice
   import dependence?** Resolves with sector-specific research directly comparing local paddy
   quality, processing capacity, and consumer preference against imports.
6. **What is the outcome of the NAFCO corruption allegations reported in October 2025?** Resolves
   once the case reaches a public disposition.
7. **Does Ghana have a current, evaluated market information system helping farmers make planting
   and selling decisions?** Not established in this research pass; a genuine gap, not an assumption
   either way.

## 10. Policy recommendations

1. **Publish one reconciled national food-insecurity figure**, with each underlying measure (IFC,
   hunger, emergency/crisis caseload) explicitly defined side by side rather than left to compete
   for headlines. Actor: Ghana Statistical Service, with WFP and AGRA. Done: a single annual report
   defining and comparing all three measures.
2. **Scale proven low-cost storage technology beyond pilot stage.** The CoolBot-style cold storage
   trial that cut onion losses from 30% to 5% should have a funded, public rollout plan. Actor:
   Ministry of Food and Agriculture, with NAFCO. Done: a published rollout plan naming a target
   number of facilities and a timeline, not another pilot study.
3. **Resolve the Pwalugu Tomato Factory's status definitively.** Either fund its revival with a
   public completion timeline, or formally redirect the investment and say so. Actor: Ministry of
   Trade and Industry, with Ministry of Food and Agriculture. Done: a public decision, with a funded
   timeline if reviving.
4. **Independently audit and publicly resolve the NAFCO corruption allegations.** Actor:
   Auditor-General or Office of the Special Prosecutor. Done: a public case-resolution report.
5. **Commission an independent midpoint evaluation of PFJ 2.0** against its stated five-year food
   security and resilience objectives, rather than waiting until the programme ends to assess it.
   Actor: an independent academic or think-tank body (e.g., IFPRI Ghana), commissioned by MoFA or
   Parliament. Done: a published midpoint evaluation report.
6. **Build or revive a functioning, evaluated warehouse receipt system**, since existing references
   found in this research are stale. Actor: Bank of Ghana, with NAFCO. Done: a current, dated,
   public report on the system's operational status and farmer uptake.
7. **Treat northern chronic food insecurity and southern glut-and-waste as two explicitly separate
   strategies**, not one undifferentiated food security plan, given how differently concentrated
   and structurally distinct they are. Actor: Ministry of Food and Agriculture, National Development
   Planning Commission, and Ghana Statistical Service, jointly. Done: two clearly separated strategy
   documents, or one document with two clearly separated strategies inside it.
