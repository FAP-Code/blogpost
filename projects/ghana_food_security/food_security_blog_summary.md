# Ghana's Food Paradox: What We Actually Know

*A plain-language breakdown of the full research report on food security and the food glut paradox
in Ghana. For the full analysis with sources and caveats, see `food_security_full_report.md`; this
is the short version.*

---

## Theme 1: Ghana isn't short of food, it's short of a working system to move it

In 2025, farmers across Ghana were sitting on 1.2 million metric tonnes of unsold grain, including
roughly $330 million worth of unsold rice. At the same time, about 8 million Ghanaians, close to a
quarter of the country, didn't have enough food to eat, a figure that's risen more than 50% since
2023. A separate government-linked figure puts the number as high as 12.5 million. These aren't the
same measurement, and no one source explains exactly how they relate, but even the lower one is a
strikingly large number to coexist with a literal grain surplus.

The clearest sentence explaining this comes from the researchers who documented it: national
harvests are surging, but the last mile, actually getting that food to people who can afford it, is
failing. This isn't a farming problem. It's a plumbing problem.

**Bottom line:** Ghana grows enough food. The system for storing it, moving it, and pricing it so
both farmers and consumers can survive the transaction is what's broken.

---

## Theme 2: This is genuinely two different crises wearing one name

Go to Ashanti or Bono region and the problem is tomatoes rotting in the field because there's
nowhere to store them and cheaper tomatoes are crossing the border from Burkina Faso. Go to
northern Ghana and the problem looks completely different: chronic poverty, and now refugees
fleeing conflict in Burkina Faso straining already thin resources. The north holds 56% of Ghana's
food-insecure people and nearly all of the most severe, emergency-level cases, while being home to
just 28% of the population.

Lumping these together as one national "food crisis" would mean building cold storage in a region
that needs poverty relief, or sending emergency food aid to a region that actually needs a working
market. They need different fixes because they're different problems.

**Bottom line:** the south has a glut-and-waste problem. The north has a chronic poverty problem.
Solving one does almost nothing for the other.

---

## Theme 3: The fixes mostly already exist, they're just not being scaled or finished

A field trial using a simple cold-storage system cut onion losses from 30% down to 5%, proof that
the technology to fix this isn't the missing piece. What's missing is scaling it up. Ghana's
flagship tomato-processing factory, built decades ago specifically to solve this exact problem, has
sat stalled and underused for years despite repeated public calls to revive it, with one industry
expert claiming it could process the country's entire tomato harvest in just three months if it
were actually running.

Even the institution built specifically to buy up surplus grain and stabilize prices for farmers
has its own serious problem: a 2025 investigation documented a corruption scandal inside it,
described as having crippled its ability to do the job it exists for.

**Bottom line:** this isn't a story about Ghana lacking answers. It's a story about pilot programs
that never scaled, a factory that never got fixed, and a stabilization agency undermined from
inside.

---

## Theme 4: Some past efforts fixed the wrong half of the problem

Ghana's flagship farming support program in the 2010s genuinely worked at raising how much farmers
grew, maize yields jumped roughly two-thirds in a single year, and it added over a million extra
tonnes of grain nationally within a few years. But the same research that credits it for that also
found it focused so heavily on getting farmers to grow more that it didn't invest nearly as much in
helping them store, process, or sell what they grew. In other words, it may have made the glut side
of the problem bigger without doing much for the storage and market side that would have absorbed
it.

A second phase of that program is now underway with stated goals to fix food security within five
years, but no independent check on whether it's actually working has been done yet.

**Bottom line:** growing more food isn't the same as fixing the system that turns extra food into
food security. Ghana has, at times, gotten good at the first and neglected the second.
