# The Politics of Fuel Pricing in Ghana: Deregulation on Paper, Politics at the Pump

**Prepared:** 2026-08-03, via `WebSearch` only in a single working session. Every figure carries a
verification status tag (`officially_confirmed`, `independently_reported`, `political_claim`, or
`disputed`) and a source with an access date. Where sources disagree, both are shown; see the
Discrepancy Log before citing any figure from this report elsewhere.

**Core finding, stated plainly:** Ghana's 2015 fuel price deregulation genuinely removed the
government from setting the pump price day to day, and the evidence supports that roughly 75 to
81% of the price (crude oil plus the cedi exchange rate) is now structurally outside any Ghanaian
government's control. But two things complicate the "deregulated" label: first, government retains
real, active levers over the remaining 15 to 20% (levies, margins, and a 2024 price floor policy),
and uses them, sometimes to raise revenue, sometimes to cut prices under public pressure; second, a
peer-reviewed study finds that politicians on all sides continue to campaign on fuel-price promises
their own structural analysis shows they mostly cannot keep, a political pattern independent of
which party is in office. The report treats both findings as equally real rather than picking one
narrative over the other.

---

## 1. Executive summary

Ghana moved to an Automatic Petroleum Product Pricing Formula (APPPF) in mid-2015, ending a system
where the National Petroleum Authority (NPA) directly negotiated prices with importers every two
weeks. Under APPPF, Oil Marketing Companies (OMCs) and Bulk Import Distribution and Export
Companies (BIDECs) set prices themselves, twice a month, based on a formula built from world crude
prices and the cedi-to-dollar exchange rate, averaged over the prior two-week window.
`officially_confirmed`

The ex-refinery price (crude plus landing or local production cost) makes up about 75% of the
petrol price and 81% of the diesel price. Taxes and levies account for about 17% (petrol) and 14%
(diesel). Margins for BIDECs, OMCs, and dealers make up the remaining 8% and 6% respectively.
`independently_reported` (see Section 3 for the full breakdown and a rounding note)

Despite the "deregulated" label, government retains two concrete tools that shape the price: the
five levies under the Energy Sector Levies Act, 2025 (Act 1135), which Parliament actively amends
(most recently 2026-08-01, raising one levy nearly eightfold), and a price floor policy introduced
in April 2024 that sets a legal minimum retail price, a policy a practicing lawyer has publicly
argued contradicts deregulation's own logic. `officially_confirmed`

A 2024 peer-reviewed study (Yeboah-Assiamah, Kundi, and Asamoah, *Politics & Policy*) is the single
most important source for this report's core question. It finds that Ghanaian political parties
gain real electoral leverage by promising lower fuel prices while campaigning, then fail to deliver
once in office, not primarily because of bad faith, but because the government's actual influence
over the price is limited to a fraction of it. The study frames this through Miles' Law ("where you
stand depends on where you sit"): the same politicians who criticize fuel prices from opposition
defend the same structural constraints once in government. This pattern is visible in real time in
2026, when a former ruling-party official's public taunt about fuel prices mirrors, almost word for
word, a campaign promise the opposite party made in 2016. `independently_reported`

On the levy and subsidy question specifically: the official position is that broad consumer fuel
subsidies ended with deregulation in 2015. The evidence only partly supports a clean version of that
claim. A reported GH₵2.00-per-litre diesel "subsidy buffer" appears in April 2026 reporting, and a
2026-08-01 law reforms how a targeted industrial subsidy (for thermal power plant fuel, among
others) is administered, moving from upfront exemption to after-the-fact reimbursement,
specifically because the upfront system was reportedly being abused. A subsidy mechanism, narrower
and more targeted than the pre-2015 system but real, still exists inside the framework. `disputed`

On consumer and business impact: transport fares are the clearest, most immediate transmission
channel. The Ghana Private Road Transport Union (GPRTU) raised fares 20% in June 2026, with the
Chamber of Petroleum Consumers (COPEC) publicly endorsing the increase as reasonable given fuel
costs; by July 2026, GPRTU proposed a further 30% increase, which COPEC then publicly pushed back
on as excessive. This shows consumer advocacy and the transport sector are not a single unified
bloc, they agree fuel costs justify some fare increases and disagree on how much. `officially_confirmed`
/ `independently_reported`

## 2. Background: from negotiated prices to a formula

Before July 2015, the NPA negotiated petroleum prices directly with importers, distributors, and
marketers roughly every two weeks, and government decided how much subsidy to offer, meaning the
price consumers paid was the underlying cost minus whatever subsidy the government chose to fund
that period. `independently_reported`

The Automatic Petroleum Product Pricing Formula replaced this system in mid-June 2015 (one source
gives July 2015 as the effective date; see Discrepancy Log item 1). Politically, this shift was
entangled with the 2015 introduction of a 17.5% Special Petroleum Tax by the government of the day,
and fuel prices became a defining issue of the 2016 election campaign, in which the eventual winner
campaigned partly on a promise to bring prices down. `independently_reported`

**What deregulation actually changed:** OMCs and BIDECs now set prices themselves within the
formula, rather than the NPA setting them directly. **What deregulation did not remove:** the
NPA's supervisory role (checking that OMCs and BIDECs price in conformity with the formula), the
government's levy-setting power through Parliament, and, since April 2024, a floor beneath which no
OMC may legally price. `officially_confirmed`

## 3. The pricing formula, explained, with a litre price breakdown

Prices are reviewed in two windows per month (the 1st to 15th, and the 16th to the end of the
month). World crude prices and the cedi exchange rate, which both move daily, are averaged across
the prior two-week window and fed into the formula for the coming window. `officially_confirmed`

| Component | Share of petrol price | Share of diesel price | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ex-refinery price (world crude + landing/production cost) | ~75% | ~81% | `independently_reported` |
| Taxes and levies | ~17% | ~14% | `independently_reported` |
| BIDEC, OMC, and dealer margins | ~8% | ~6% | `independently_reported` |
| **Total** | **100%** | **101%** | rounding, see Discrepancy Log item 2 |

**The five levies currently in force**, under the Energy Sector Levies Act, 2025 (Act 1135):
the Energy Sector Shortfall and Debt Repayment Levy, the Road Fund Levy, the Energy Fund Levy, the
Public Lighting Levy, and the National Electrification Scheme Levy. `officially_confirmed`

**The 2026-08-01 amendment**, passed to close a fraud loophole, raised the fuel-oil rate of the
Energy Sector Shortfall and Debt Repayment Levy from GH¢0.24 to GH¢1.93 per litre (roughly an
eightfold increase, bringing fuel oil in line with the rate already charged on diesel and marine
gas oil) and extended the Road Fund Levy to cover fuel oil as well. Finance Minister Cassiel Ato
Forson stated this is not a new tax but a reform of how an existing subsidy is administered,
specifically replacing an upfront exemption with an after-the-fact reimbursement model, to close
an abuse loophole where some beneficiaries had reportedly exploited the exemption for private
gain. `officially_confirmed`

**Illustrative recent price-floor levels** (bi-monthly windows, 2026): petrol floor rose from
GH¢13.28 to GH¢14.53 per litre between one window and the next in July 2026; diesel floor rose
from GH¢14.35 to GH¢16.97 over the same period, a steeper absolute jump for the fuel that powers
most commercial transport. Separately, an April 2026 report put the diesel price at GH¢17.10 per
litre alongside a reported GH¢2.00-per-litre subsidy buffer. `officially_confirmed`

**Why a price floor exists inside a "deregulated" system:** the NPA introduced the Petroleum Price
Floor Programme in April 2024, explaining it as protection against a price war in which large,
well-capitalized OMCs could underprice small, community-level fuel stations out of business,
concentrating the market and eventually letting the survivors raise prices unchecked; it is also
described as protecting banks that hold loans to smaller fuel companies. `independently_reported`.
This is directly in tension with deregulation's own logic, and at least one lawyer has publicly and
specifically called for the floor's abolition on exactly those grounds. `political_claim` (the
floor's policy purpose, as officially stated) / `independently_reported` (that the criticism exists)

## 4. Deregulation in practice: where the formula meets politics

**The central, best-evidenced finding of this research** is the 2024 peer-reviewed study by
Yeboah-Assiamah, Kundi, and Asamoah (*Politics & Policy*, 52(6), 1353 to 1376), based on content
analysis and interviews with energy experts. It finds that Ghanaian political parties gain
significant electoral leverage by promising lower fuel prices while campaigning, then fail to
deliver once in office, largely because the government's actual influence over the pump price is
minimal, most of the price is set by factors (world crude, the cedi) no Ghanaian government
directly controls. The study explicitly invokes Miles' Law ("where you stand depends on where you
sit") to describe how the same rhetoric flips depending on whether a party is in government or
opposition, and recommends improving fiscal and exchange-rate stability and better public education
on what actually drives the price, rather than continued promise-and-blame cycling. `independently_reported`

This pattern is visible in real time and across both major parties, which is why this report treats
it as structural rather than partisan:

- **2015 to 2016**: the government introduced a 17.5% Special Petroleum Tax in the 2015 budget; the
  eventual 2016 election winner had campaigned partly on bringing fuel prices down, and price
  surges under the new government afterward were reported as being in tension with that promise.
  `independently_reported`
- **2026**: a former ruling-party national chairman publicly taunted the sitting government,
  saying that if he were the president he would "slash fuel prices back to the good old days," the
  same rhetorical move, aimed in the opposite partisan direction, a decade later. `political_claim`

**Where government does have real, documented leverage** is the levy and margin share of the price,
not the crude/cedi share, and 2026 provides a clear example of this lever being pulled under public
pressure. President Mahama directed the Ministries of Energy and Finance to review the full price
build-up and recommend reductions in taxes, margins, and levies. In response, a coalition of IMANI
Africa, COPEC, INSTEPR, and the Institute for Energy Security (IES) jointly proposed a cumulative
GH₵1.65 reduction in the price build-up, sustained for two months, rather than the four-week window
the government had reportedly proposed. `independently_reported`. What became of this specific
proposal (whether adopted, modified, or shelved) was not established in this research pass; see
Section 9.

**The price floor is the clearest documented case of formal deregulation and administered-price
reality sitting in open tension**, not hidden, but openly debated: it is publicly defended by the
NPA on market-stability grounds and publicly attacked by at least one legal commentator as
contradicting deregulation outright. `independently_reported`

## 5. The levy and subsidy question

The official position is straightforward: Ghana ended broad, universal fuel subsidies with the 2015
deregulation. The evidence supports a narrower, more complicated version of that claim:

1. **The five ESLA levies are a tax, not a subsidy**, in the sense that they add to the price to
   fund specific programmes (thermal plant fuel supply, road maintenance, electrification, public
   lighting) rather than reduce it. `officially_confirmed`
2. **A targeted, ad hoc diesel subsidy buffer (GH₵2.00 per litre) was reported for April 2026**,
   which is not consistent with a claim that all fuel subsidies ended in 2015. `independently_reported`,
   not reconciled with the "no more subsidies" framing by any source found; see Discrepancy Log item 3.
3. **The 2026-08-01 law reforms, rather than eliminates, a subsidy mechanism** for specific
   industrial beneficiaries (thermal plants and similar qualifying users), moving it from an
   upfront exemption to an after-the-fact reimbursement specifically because the upfront version
   was reportedly abused for private gain. `officially_confirmed`

**Who benefits from opacity in this system, a concrete, current example**: in November 2025,
Ghana's Office of the Special Prosecutor froze assets worth over GH₵100 million and US$100,000
linked to an alleged extortion and money-laundering scheme at the NPA itself, with former NPA Chief
Executive Dr. Mustapha Abdul-Hamid and nine others accused of diverting more than GH₵291 million and
US$332,000 between 2022 and 2024. `officially_confirmed` (that assets were frozen and charges
exist) / the truth of the underlying allegations is `disputed` pending any court outcome, not yet
found in this research.

A separate, smaller transparency gap: Ghana's Auditor-General found discrepancies between the
volumes of petroleum products marked (a quality- and tax-assurance process) and the volumes
eventually distributed; the NPA attributed the gap to differences in reporting classification and
to the July 2025 suspension of differentiated marking for RON 95 premium petrol.
`officially_confirmed` (that a discrepancy was found and an explanation offered) / whether that
explanation fully accounts for the gap is `disputed`, not independently verified in this research.

## 6. Consumer and business impact

**Transport fares are the clearest, fastest, and most politically visible pass-through channel.**
GPRTU raised fares 20% nationwide effective 2026-06-02, citing fuel and rising operational costs;
COPEC, generally understood as a consumer advocacy body, publicly backed the increase as reasonable
given documented fuel price rises, alongside the consumer group CUTS International.
`officially_confirmed` / `independently_reported`

By July 2026, GPRTU proposed a further 30% increase. This time COPEC pushed back, calling for
15 to 20% instead and asking GPRTU to reconsider, evidence that COPEC's earlier endorsement was
tied to the specific fuel-cost evidence at the time, not a blanket alignment with the transport
union. `independently_reported`. In the other direction, an April 2026 fuel price cut (linked to
the government's levy-review response described in Section 4) led transport operators to suspend a
previously planned fare increase, a direct, dated example of a price cut translating into fare
relief. `independently_reported`

**Business and agro-processing impact** is directionally well supported but not precisely
quantified in this research pass. General commentary confirms fuel costs feed directly into
manufacturing input costs and Ghana's 2026 inflation outlook. A peer-reviewed paper exists
specifically on this question, "The effect of fuel prices on agri-food sector output in Ghana"
(Cogent Economics & Finance, 2024, via `tandfonline.com`), directly relevant given this site's prior
work on Ghana's agricultural value chains, but this research pass located only the paper's
existence and title, not its specific quantitative findings. This is flagged as an honest gap
rather than filled in with an invented figure; see Section 9.

## 7. Political economy synthesis

Four distinct pressures interact in this space, and none of them map cleanly onto a single
political party:

1. **Structural price drivers** (world crude, the cedi exchange rate) that no Ghanaian government
   controls day to day, and that make up 75 to 81% of the pump price.
2. **Government revenue needs**, met through the five ESLA levies, which Parliament actively raises
   when needed (as in the 2026-08-01 amendment) regardless of which party is in office.
3. **Cost-of-living and political pressure**, which pushes governments to use the one lever they do
   control (levies, margins, and occasionally targeted subsidies or floor-price policy) even though
   it only affects a fraction of the total price.
4. **Organized sector actors with their own, sometimes conflicting, interests**: GPRTU (wants fares
   to track costs), COPEC (broadly consumer-aligned but willing to both back and resist fare
   increases depending on the evidence), IMANI Africa and IES (generally push for lower levies and
   more transparency), and the NPA and Ministry of Finance (balance revenue needs, market stability,
   and political pressure).

The single most important synthesis point, grounded in the Yeboah-Assiamah et al. (2024) study, is
that **the political fight over fuel prices is largely a fight over a small share of the price**,
and that both major parties have, at different points, campaigned as though they controlled the
whole of it. This is a structural pattern that outlasts any single administration, not a defect
specific to one party or government.

## 8. Discrepancy log

1. **Deregulation effective date.** "Mid-June 2015" vs. "July 2015" cited across sources for the
   same policy shift. Unresolved.
2. **Price-component percentages.** Petrol's three components (75% + 17% + 8%) sum to exactly
   100%; diesel's (81% + 14% + 6%) sum to 101%. Likely rounding in the original source; not
   independently recalculated in this research pass.
3. **"No fuel subsidies since 2015" vs. a reported GH₵2.00-per-litre diesel subsidy buffer (April
   2026) vs. the 2026-08-01 law's reform of an existing industrial subsidy's administration.**
   These three signals are not reconciled into one clear statement by any source found; see
   Section 5.
4. **Levy naming.** "Fuel Oil Levy" and "Energy Sector Shortfall and Debt Repayment Levy" are used
   interchangeably across outlets for the same GH¢0.24-to-GH¢1.93 change. Likely the same levy
   under an informal versus formal name, not verified against the statute text directly.
5. **Transport fare increases.** 20% (June 2026) and a separate, later, 30% proposal (July 2026)
   are sometimes reported without making clear these are two sequential, distinct events rather
   than the same figure reported inconsistently.
6. **The IMANI/COPEC GH₵1.65 reduction proposal's outcome.** Not established whether the
   government adopted the coalition's two-month timeframe, its own four-week proposal, or some
   other outcome. Unresolved.

## 10. Policy recommendations

1. **Publish a standard, itemized price build-up every pricing window**, not just the final
   ex-pump price. Actor: NPA. Done: a recurring public template, every two weeks, itemizing crude
   cost, cedi effect, each of the five levies, and margins, in plain language.
2. **Formally reconcile the price floor with deregulation, rather than leaving both to coexist
   unremarked.** Actor: NPA and Ministry of Energy. Done: a public policy statement stating the
   floor's rationale, evidence for its effectiveness so far, and a defined review date.
3. **Independently audit the ex-post subsidy reimbursement scheme**, given the documented history
   of abuse in the prior upfront system it replaced. Actor: Auditor-General, with the Ministry of
   Finance. Done: an annual, published audit of subsidy reimbursement claims and approvals.
4. **Bring the Special Prosecutor's NPA case to a public resolution** and publish the outcome.
   Actor: Office of the Special Prosecutor. Done: a public case-disposition report.
5. **Fund a repeatable, independent fuel-price pass-through study** covering transport fares and
   agro-processing input costs specifically. Actor: Ghana Statistical Service, in partnership with
   the authors of the existing 2024 agri-food fuel-price study. Done: an annual, published
   pass-through index, not a one-off academic paper.
6. **Require a standard "what government can and can't change" disclosure alongside campaign
   fuel-price promises.** Actor: a civil-society fact-checking coalition (several already operate in
   Ghana) or the Electoral Commission. Done: a citable, standard fact box, informed by this report's
   own price-formula breakdown, attached to any campaign promise about fuel prices.
