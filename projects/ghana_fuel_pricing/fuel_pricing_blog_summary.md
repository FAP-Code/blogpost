# Ghana's Fuel Prices: What We Actually Know

*A plain-language breakdown of the full research report on the politics of fuel pricing in Ghana.
For the full analysis with sources and caveats, see `fuel_pricing_full_report.md`; this is the
short version.*

---

## Theme 1: Deregulation is real, but it only covers part of the price

Ghana moved to an automatic fuel pricing formula in 2015, ending a system where government
negotiated prices directly. Under the new system, world crude prices and the cedi exchange rate,
averaged over a two-week window, make up roughly 75 to 81% of what you pay at the pump. That part
genuinely isn't set by politicians anymore.

But the remaining 15 to 20%, taxes, levies, and margins, is still very much a government lever, and
it gets used. Parliament raised one fuel levy nearly eightfold in a single amendment passed on
August 1, 2026. And since April 2024, the government has also set a legal minimum price, a floor
below which fuel can't legally be sold, which a practicing lawyer has publicly argued contradicts
deregulation's own logic.

**Bottom line:** Ghana's fuel market is deregulated for most of the price and actively managed for
the rest. Both things are true at once, and treating fuel pricing as either "fully free market" or
"secretly controlled" misses the actual, more interesting split.

---

## Theme 2: The real political story isn't secret price-fixing, it's broken promises

Here's the finding that best answers the question of what's actually going on: a 2024 university
study looked at how Ghanaian politicians talk about fuel prices and found that political parties
routinely promise to bring prices down while campaigning, then fail to deliver once in office, not
mainly because they're lying, but because most of the price is genuinely outside their control once
they're actually in government.

The study calls this "where you stand depends on where you sit," and it plays out in real time
across parties. In 2016, a winning candidate campaigned partly on lowering fuel prices, then faced
the same price pressures in office. In 2026, the exact same move happened with the parties reversed:
a former ruling-party official publicly taunted the new government to "slash fuel prices back to
the good old days," almost word for word the kind of promise made a decade earlier by the other
side.

**Bottom line:** this isn't a story about one party manipulating prices. It's a structural pattern
where campaign promises about fuel prices routinely outrun what any government can actually
deliver, regardless of who's in charge.

---

## Theme 3: "No more subsidies" isn't quite the full picture

The official line is that broad fuel subsidies ended with 2015's deregulation. The evidence tells a
more complicated story. A targeted diesel subsidy of GH₵2.00 per litre showed up in reporting from
April 2026. And a law passed in August 2026 reformed, rather than eliminated, a subsidy for specific
industrial users like thermal power plants, shifting it from an upfront exemption to a
pay-first-then-get-reimbursed model, specifically because the old system was reportedly being
abused by some beneficiaries for private gain.

There's a real accountability angle here too. In November 2025, Ghana's own anti-corruption
prosecutor froze over GH₵100 million in assets linked to an alleged extortion scheme at the fuel
regulator itself, with the former head of the agency among those accused of diverting hundreds of
millions of cedis. That case's outcome isn't known yet.

**Bottom line:** subsidies didn't fully disappear in 2015, they got narrower and more targeted, and
the system built to manage them has documented, serious accountability problems of its own.

---

## Theme 4: Fuel prices hit transport fares first, and even consumer groups don't always agree

The clearest, fastest way fuel prices show up in ordinary life is transport fares. In June 2026,
Ghana's transport union raised fares by 20%, and the country's main consumer group backed the
increase as reasonable given fuel costs. But when the union proposed a further 30% increase just a
month later, that same consumer group pushed back, calling for something closer to 15 to 20%
instead.

That matters because it shows fuel-price politics in Ghana isn't a simple two-sided fight.
Consumer advocates and transport operators agree fuel costs justify some fare increases and
disagree, sometimes publicly, on exactly how much. And when a government-driven price cut happened
in April 2026, transport operators actually shelved a planned fare increase in response, real,
recent proof that this pass-through runs in both directions.

**Bottom line:** fuel prices translate into transport fares fast and visibly, but the actors
involved (consumer groups, transport unions, government) don't line up into simple sides, they
shift position based on the specific numbers in front of them at the time.
