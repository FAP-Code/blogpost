date: "2026-08-07"

The standard story of global vaccine inequity during COVID-19 is a rich-country/poor-country gap: wealthy nations bought up early supply, poorer ones waited. That story is true, but it undersells what happened to a country like Ghana, which didn't just face the global gap — it lagged behind the average of its own income bracket.

Ghana began vaccinating in March 2021. By July — as the Delta variant was becoming the dominant global strain — only 1.19% of Ghanaians were fully vaccinated. The average lower-middle-income country, Ghana's own World Bank classification, was already at 3.46%. High-income countries were at 35.18%, thirty times Ghana's rate.

The gap didn't close over the year. By late December 2021, Ghana had reached 6.97% fully vaccinated — the average lower-middle-income country was at 32.4%, and high-income countries were approaching 68.5%. Ghana ended the year vaccinated at roughly a fifth the rate of its income peers, and a tenth the rate of wealthy countries.

This matters for how the vaccine-equity story gets told. "Poor countries got less" is a supply story — it points to hoarding, patents, COVAX underdelivery. "Ghana got less than other poor countries" is a different, structural story: distribution logistics, cold-chain capacity, health-system absorption, and administrative coordination, the same kind of institutional bottleneck this blog has argued limits Ghana's non-traditional export value chains. Vaccine access wasn't only about who had doses to give; it was also about who had the systems in place to get them into arms once they arrived.

One honest caveat, because the underlying research doesn't stop at rollout speed: a broader analysis across 65 countries found no statistically significant relationship between a country's vaccination coverage in mid-2021 and its mortality during the subsequent Delta wave (p = 0.32). That's not evidence vaccines didn't work — individual-level clinical evidence on that is extensive and settled — it's a reminder that country-level aggregate data is a blunt instrument for that particular question, for reasons the full write-up goes into. The equity finding above is a separate, much more robust result (wealth predicted vaccination coverage at r = 0.61, p < 0.001).

The full analysis — methodology, regression results, and figures — is in the [`covid19-vaccine-effectiveness`](https://github.com/FAP-Code/datascience/tree/claude/datascience-projects-repo-emigej/projects/covid19-vaccine-effectiveness) project in the data science repo, built on Our World in Data's COVID-19 dataset.
