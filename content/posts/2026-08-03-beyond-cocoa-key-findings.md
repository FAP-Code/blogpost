*Beyond Cocoa: Unlocking Ghana's Non-Traditional Agricultural Value Chains for Diversification and Inclusive Growth* is the full policy paper behind this blog (the source document is in `research/beyond-cocoa/` in this repo). This post pulls out the findings and figures that matter most, with their sources, for readers who want the substance without the full paper.

## Ghana is swapping one raw export for another, not diversifying

Gold now accounts for about 47% of merchandise export earnings, having overtaken cocoa (around 20%) as the country's largest export. But gold mining employs fewer than 4% of the workforce and is exposed to the same boom-bust price cycles that made cocoa dependence risky in the first place (Ghana Statistical Service, 2024; World Bank, 2024).

Non-traditional agricultural exports, cashew, shea, rubber, oil palm, mango, and pineapple, together make up only about 6% of exports today, despite employing far more people per dollar of output than gold or crude oil.

The gap between raw export price and processed price is large and quantifiable: Ghana sells raw cashew nuts at roughly $0.80 to $1.00 per kilogram and imports processed cashew kernel back at $7 to $10 per kilogram, a $6 to $9 loss in value per kilogram, multiplied across 250,000 tonnes of annual production (TCDA, 2026).

## The gap is governance, not capital

If the constraint were really capital, the fix would be straightforward: processing plants would sit empty or unbuilt for lack of financing, and more investment would close the gap. That is not what the record shows in any of the three cases below. In each one, either the processing capacity already exists and can't get fed, or a government already has the policy lever available and simply hasn't pulled it. That distinction, capacity blocked by a rule or an unenforced decision, rather than capacity that was never financed in the first place, is what "governance, not capital" is actually claiming, and it's testable case by case:

- **Cashew**: Usibras, Ghana's largest cashew processor, is a real, already-built factory, the capital to process cashew domestically exists. It considered relocating to Côte d'Ivoire after nearly a decade of operating below capacity, not because it ran out of money, but because raw material kept leaving the country first: export tariff structures reward raw exports over local processing, so foreign buyers can outbid Usibras for the same nuts at the farm gate before it can secure supply (IMANI Africa, 2025). A tariff schedule is a policy choice, not a financing gap; fixing it requires changing the incentive, not writing a bigger check.
- **Shea**: this is the clearest case, because the policy lever is visible and simply wasn't used. When 2024 production fell 45% below projections, the government still permitted exporters to ship nearly half the available nuts rather than tightening allocation toward domestic processors to protect their supply. That single permitting decision, not a lack of investment in shea processing, is what let shea nut prices spike 140% between 2023 and mid-2024 and pushed shea butter output at processors like Organic Trade and Investments down more than 60% (OTI-GATI, 2025). The raw material was there; the allocation rule that would have kept more of it in-country for processing wasn't enforced.
- **Oil palm**: Ghana already grows the crop domestically, so this isn't a case of capital never arriving to plant it. The gap is that Ghana still imports refined palm products instead of processing more of its own crude oil, and smallholder yields are stuck at about a quarter of estate-level yields for lack of improved planting material and extension services (FAO, 2013), the kind of ongoing technical support that is a government service-delivery function, not a one-time capital outlay a bigger loan would solve.

## The opportunity on the table

The Tree Crops Development Authority's 2026 blueprint projects that Ghana's six priority tree crops could generate **$12 billion annually by 2030** if processing targets are met, compared to well under $1 billion in non-traditional agricultural export revenue today (TCDA, 2026). Reaching that requires institutional coordination as much as investment: the paper's recommendations include mandatory domestic processing floors for cashew, an enforced shea export ban paired with a price stabilisation fund, and a coordination office with authority over the ministries and agencies (MoFA, MOTI, GEPA, TCDA, and the Development Bank Ghana) that currently operate independently of each other.
