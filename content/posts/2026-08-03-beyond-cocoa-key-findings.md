*Beyond Cocoa: Unlocking Ghana's Non-Traditional Agricultural Value Chains for Diversification and Inclusive Growth* is the full policy paper behind this blog (the source document is in `research/beyond-cocoa/` in this repo). This post pulls out the findings and figures that matter most, with their sources, for readers who want the substance without the full paper.

## Ghana is swapping one raw export for another, not diversifying

Gold now accounts for about 47% of merchandise export earnings, having overtaken cocoa (around 20%) as the country's largest export. But gold mining employs fewer than 4% of the workforce and is exposed to the same boom-bust price cycles that made cocoa dependence risky in the first place (Ghana Statistical Service, 2024; World Bank, 2024).

Non-traditional agricultural exports, cashew, shea, rubber, oil palm, mango, and pineapple, together make up only about 6% of exports today, despite employing far more people per dollar of output than gold or crude oil.

## Almost none of it is processed at home

The gap between raw export price and processed price is large and quantifiable: Ghana sells raw cashew nuts at roughly $0.80 to $1.00 per kilogram and imports processed cashew kernel back at $7 to $10 per kilogram, a $6 to $9 loss in value per kilogram, multiplied across 250,000 tonnes of annual production (TCDA, 2026).

## The gap is governance, not capital

Three concrete cases from the paper:

- **Cashew**: Usibras, Ghana's largest cashew processor, considered relocating to Côte d'Ivoire after nearly a decade of operating below capacity due to raw material shortages, high energy costs, and tariff structures that reward raw exports over local processing (IMANI Africa, 2025).
- **Shea**: in the 2024 season, production fell 45% below projections, yet exporters were still permitted to ship nearly half the available nuts. The price of a 100kg bag of shea nuts rose 140% between 2023 and mid-2024, and shea butter output from processors like Organic Trade and Investments fell by more than 60% (OTI-GATI, 2025).
- **Oil palm**: Ghana grows palm oil domestically but still imports refined palm products, spending foreign exchange on a commodity it could process at home. Smallholder oil palm yields are about a quarter of estate-level yields (FAO, 2013).

## The opportunity on the table

The Tree Crops Development Authority's 2026 blueprint projects that Ghana's six priority tree crops could generate **$12 billion annually by 2030** if processing targets are met, compared to well under $1 billion in non-traditional agricultural export revenue today (TCDA, 2026). Reaching that requires institutional coordination as much as investment: the paper's recommendations include mandatory domestic processing floors for cashew, an enforced shea export ban paired with a price stabilisation fund, and a coordination office with authority over the ministries and agencies (MoFA, MOTI, GEPA, TCDA, and the Development Bank Ghana) that currently operate independently of each other.
