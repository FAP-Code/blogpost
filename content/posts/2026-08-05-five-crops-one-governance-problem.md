*Beyond Cocoa* builds its argument around five case studies. Each crop has its own agronomy and history, but the same structural pattern shows up in every one: productive capacity exists, foreign buyers offer cash that domestic processors cannot match, factories run below capacity, and a policy response gets announced but not enforced.

## Cashew: the feedstock dilemma, in one company

Ghana produces about 250,000 tonnes of raw cashew a year, with the TCDA targeting 600,000 tonnes by 2030. Only 6 to 10% is processed domestically; the rest goes raw to India and Vietnam, where it's turned into kernels, butter, and industrial shell liquid and sold at three to four times the farm-gate price (TCDA, 2026; Ministry of Food and Agriculture, 2025).

The clearest illustration is Usibras, Ghana's largest cashew processor. After nearly a decade of operating below capacity, it considered relocating to Côte d'Ivoire, citing chronic raw material shortages, high industrial energy costs, and export tariffs that inadvertently reward raw exports over local processing (IMANI Africa, 2025). As the paper puts it: a country's largest processor contemplating exit isn't a market signal, it's a governance alarm.

## Shea: a supply base that can't expand, and a ban that isn't enforced

Shea nuts come from wild parkland trees that can't be cultivated in the conventional sense, so the supply base can't be grown through planting programmes. Ghana was the world's top shea butter exporter in 2023, earning $90 to 100 million, work carried out almost entirely by women collectors and processors in the three northern regions (News Ghana, 2025).

In the 2024 season, production fell 45% below projections, but exporters were still permitted to ship nearly half the available nuts. Shea nut prices rose 140% between 2023 and mid-2024, artisanal processors couldn't compete, and shea butter output at firms like Organic Trade and Investments fell by more than 60% (OTI-GATI, 2025). The government's announced ban on raw shea exports has not been consistently enforced.

## Rubber: the same dilemma, plus an energy cost problem

Ghana's rubber sector produces about 145,000 tonnes annually and exports roughly 95% of it as raw latex, forgoing the margin on rubber sheets, technically specified rubber, and derivatives used in tyres, footwear, and medical equipment (Ministry of Food and Agriculture, 2025). Processing needs reliable, affordable electricity for compound mixing and vulcanisation, and Ghana's history of power instability makes that a direct competitiveness problem. In 2025, an industry proposal for a 3 to 6 month trial suspension of raw rubber exports, to test whether local processors could absorb the supply, went unactioned (IMANI Africa, 2025).

## Horticulture: the constraint here is infrastructure, not farming

Pineapple and mango are Ghana's fastest-growing agricultural export sub-sector, with export relationships already established with European buyers for the MD2 pineapple variety (Annor et al., 2025). But post-harvest losses run 30 to 40% for some horticultural crops, mostly lost between the farm and the port because there's no functional cold chain (HortiFresh, 2021). EU rules like the Deforestation Regulation add traceability requirements that smallholders without GlobalGAP certification simply can't meet, locking them out of the premium market segments.

## Oil palm: growing the crop, importing the product

Ghana produces crude palm oil domestically but still imports refined palm products, spending foreign exchange on a commodity it has the land and agronomic conditions to process at home (FAO, 2013; IMANI Africa, 2025). Smallholder oil palm yields are about a quarter of estate-level yields, reflecting limited access to improved planting material and extension services. The One District, One Factory programme was meant to close this gap; independent research found weak institutional coordination and inadequate monitoring undermined its impact across sectors before it was officially discontinued (Amponsah et al., 2026).

## The common thread

Across all five crops, the constraint isn't investment, it's that foreign buyers can always outbid domestic processors on cash-in-hand, and no enforced policy currently changes that incentive. That's the subject of the next post: the eight specific recommendations the paper proposes to fix it.
