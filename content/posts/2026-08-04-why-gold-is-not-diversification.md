Ghana's agricultural economy was built around cocoa: a single crop, priced by buyers in London and New York, that delivered boom years when global prices rose and fiscal crisis when they fell. That vulnerability defined the country's economy for most of the twentieth century, regardless of which government was in power (Ruf, 2007).

In 2019, Ghana overtook South Africa to become Africa's largest gold producer, and gold now earns roughly 47% of merchandise export revenue, ahead of cocoa's 20% (Ghana Statistical Service, 2024). It is tempting to read this as economic progress. The paper behind this blog, *Beyond Cocoa*, argues it is closer to the same structural problem wearing different clothes.

## Gold looks like growth, but it isn't inclusive growth

Gold mining in Ghana is dominated by large, capital-intensive multinational operations. The sector employs fewer than 4% of the national workforce, generates limited demand for domestic suppliers, and produces the same fiscal pro-cyclicality that made cocoa risky: government revenue surges when prices rise and contracts sharply when they fall (World Bank, 2024). Ghana experienced exactly that whiplash in 2022, when falling commodity revenue and rising debt service costs combined to trigger the fiscal crisis that led to the IMF bailout.

Agriculture, by contrast, still employs over 40% of Ghana's workforce, with roughly four in five farmers working smallholder plots under two hectares (Farmonaut, 2025). That is the structural argument for non-traditional agricultural value chains, cashew, shea, rubber, oil palm, mango, and pineapple: they are the sectors positioned to convert growth into jobs and rural income, not just export receipts.

## Four reasons the paper gives for prioritising these crops

1. **Employment intensity.** Processing facilities, shelling plants, shea refineries, cold-chain packhouses, create jobs across production, aggregation, quality control, packaging, logistics, and marketing, not just at the farm gate.
2. **Rural livelihood impact.** Most of Ghana's poor population is rural and agriculture-dependent. Processing located near production zones raises the price farmers receive for their output, rather than leaving them as price-takers selling raw commodities.
3. **Gender inclusion.** Shea processing in northern Ghana is carried out almost entirely by women. Research from Wageningen UR found that women in cashew and shea processing groups earn substantially more than non-participants, investing the difference in housing, business expansion, and community lending (van den Berg, 2015).
4. **Genuine diversification and industrial linkages.** Processed cashew, shea butter, refined rubber, and packaged fruit do not move on the same price cycles as gold or cocoa, giving the economy a macroeconomic buffer that raw commodity dependence cannot. Processing also creates demand for packaging, cold storage, laboratory testing, and transport, the industrial base gold extraction does not build (Owoo and Lambon-Quayefio, 2017).
